The short version

Buying a NAS drive in 2026 comes down to three decisions, in this order: CMR not SMR (non-negotiable for RAID), the right tier for your workload (a home 4-bay NAS does not need an enterprise-grade drive), and the right capacity at the right price-per-terabyte (which, as you’ll see below, does not automatically favor the biggest drive). Get those three right and brand is almost a coin flip — Western Digital, Seagate, and Toshiba all make solid current-generation CMR NAS drives.

This guide leans on two things most competing listicles skip: real dollar-per-terabyte math from a dated live price pull, and the recertified-drive economics that a growing number of homelabbers use to beat the 2026 hard-drive price crunch. We have not benchmarked these drives ourselves — every reliability and workload number here comes straight from the manufacturer spec sheets, cited inline. For the box these drives go into, see our companion budget NAS build guide — or, if you’re building a media server, the best NAS for Plex and Jellyfin, where hardware transcoding rather than capacity drives the pick. This page is about the disks.

Picks by use case

Best for a 4-bay home NAS: WD Red Plus

The WD Red Plus line is purpose-built for exactly this: WD’s own product brief positions it “for RAID-optimized NAS systems with up to 8 bays.” It spans 2TB to 12TB and is all-CMR at every capacity per the WD Red Plus data sheet (Sep 2025). The core spec across the line: 180TB/year workload rate, 1,000,000-hour MTBF, and a 3-year limited warranty. RPM varies by model — the current 12TB and 10TB SKUs are 7200 RPM, while the 8TB and below run 5400/5640 RPM, which keeps the smaller drives quieter and cooler for a home shelf or closet. (Note: the specific 12TB row we priced below is the older WD120EFAX, which is the 5400-RPM variant — the current 7200-RPM 12TB is the newer WD120EFBX. Both are CMR.)

Why it wins the home slot: you get genuine NAS-rated CMR reliability without paying the Pro-tier premium, and the 180TB/year workload is plenty for a household that isn’t hammering the array around the clock. A new 8TB WD Red Plus (WD80EFZZ) ran $339.99 — $42.50/TB in our July 3 pull (Amazon).

Best for 24-7 heavier duty: Seagate IronWolf Pro or WD Red Pro

If your NAS runs constantly, hosts many users, or lives in an 8+ bay chassis, step up to the Pro tier — and note that both leaders roughly triple the workload rating of the home drives.

Seagate IronWolf Pro is an all-CMR portfolio; Seagate states plainly that “all IronWolf Pro drives utilize conventional magnetic recording (CMR) technology.” The current top capacities reach 30TB and 28TB, rated for a 550TB/year workload, 2,500,000-hour MTBF, 7200 RPM, and a 5-year limited warranty, per the IronWolf Pro data sheet. Its signature extra is 3 years of complimentary Rescue Data Recovery Services bundled in — a real safety net if a drive dies with data you never backed up (though you should back up anyway).

WD Red Pro matches the mission: all-CMR from 2TB up to 26TB, 550TB/year workload, 7200 RPM across the whole line, and a 5-year limited warranty, per the WD Red Pro data sheet (Jan 2025). MTBF is 2,500,000 hours on the 14TB-26TB models and 2,000,000 hours on the 2TB-12TB models. The largest capacities (22-26TB) use OptiNAND; the big models are helium-sealed for lower power and heat.

Between the two, the tie-breakers are price at your target capacity and whether the IronWolf Pro’s bundled Rescue service matters to you. Both are correct answers for a heavy 24-7 array. And if you’re deciding specifically between IronWolf, IronWolf Pro, and an enterprise Exos, our Seagate NAS drive breakdown settles which of the three fits your bays and budget.

Best for Plex / media bulk: the lowest-$/TB CMR drive at 8-16TB

For a media server, you are buying capacity per dollar, not raw speed — a single spinning NAS drive already saturates a typical home network, so IOPS is rarely the constraint for streaming. That flips the decision into a straight $/TB shopping exercise across the CMR families, in the 8-16TB band where value tends to concentrate.

Two honest caveats from our own price pull, both covered in the table below: (1) among new drives, bigger did not mean cheaper per-TB — the new 12TB WD Red Plus ($48.53/TB) was pricier per terabyte than the new 8TB ($42.50/TB); and (2) the outright value champion was a recertified 16TB IronWolf Pro at $31.25/TB. So for media bulk, shop the live $/TB at 8-16TB rather than assuming the biggest new drive is the best deal, and seriously consider recertified for the bulk tier. For the transcoding side of a media box, our best GPU for Plex/Jellyfin guide covers the compute half.

Best budget: a recertified big-capacity CMR drive (with eyes open)

The cheapest way to fill bays in 2026 is a recertified enterprise-class or NAS-Pro CMR drive. In our July 3 pull the recertified 16TB IronWolf Pro (ST16000NE000) was $499.99 = $31.25/TB, versus $640 = $40.00/TB for the same model listed as new — a ~22% discount and the lowest cost-per-terabyte in the entire comparison (recertified Amazon listing). (The $640 “new” anchor came from a third-party marketplace listing rather than a first-party pristine-new sale, so treat it as the same-model reference price, not a guaranteed factory-new quote.) The trade-offs are real and covered in the recertified section below: thinner warranty and thinner review counts. This is a value play for someone running redundancy and backups, not a set-and-forget pick.

For budget on a new drive, the standard-tier lines (base Seagate IronWolf, standard Toshiba N300) exist, but note Toshiba’s N300 8TB was actually the most expensive per-TB new drive in our pull at $55.59/TB — so “budget brand” does not always mean budget price at a given capacity. Shop the number, not the label.

CMR vs SMR — and the WD Red history you should know

This is the single most important technical distinction for a NAS drive, and it has a real cautionary tale attached.

CMR (conventional magnetic recording) writes data tracks side by side. SMR (shingled magnetic recording) overlaps tracks like roof shingles to pack in more areal density. Seagate frames it cleanly on its official CMR/SMR list: CMR is the plug-and-play choice for standard NAS deployments that prioritize reliability, while SMR is best only for sequential, write-once workloads (archives, backup tiers) and generally wants SMR-aware file systems. The practical NAS problem is that SMR’s device-managed garbage collection and band-rewrite behavior can drastically slow random writes and RAID rebuilds — the worst possible time for a drive to crawl is while your array is rebuilding after a failure.

The reason NAS buyers learned to check this the hard way: around 2020, plain WD Red (non-Plus) “EFAX” drives were quietly SMR. Western Digital’s own WD Red product brief lists the WD60EFAX (6TB), WD40EFAX (4TB), WD30EFAX (3TB), and WD20EFAX (2TB) as SMR, and WD’s own blog statement names the same capacities directly — “our current device-managed shingled magnetic recording (DMSMR) (2TB, 3TB, 4TB, and 6TB) WD Red series.” Those are the drives at the center of the SMR-in-NAS backlash, and they are why the “Plus” and “Pro” branding exists. WD’s official CMR/SMR determination page advises checking each drive’s data sheet to confirm whether it uses CMR or SMR — which we’ve done for you here.

The good news for 2026: every current dedicated NAS-Pro family is 100% CMR. The WD Red Plus, WD Red Pro, Seagate IronWolf and IronWolf Pro, and Toshiba N300 and N300 Pro data sheets all read CMR across every capacity, and Seagate flags zero IronWolf-family drives as SMR on its product list. So the practical rule is simple: buy a current NAS-Pro CMR drive and steer clear of the legacy EFAX SKUs on the used market.

Recertified drives — what the seller pages actually say

Recertified and renewed drives are the biggest under-covered angle in NAS-drive buying, and the price crunch of 2026 has made them mainstream. Here is what’s real, stated straight from seller documentation rather than forum lore.

The discount is real: across two clean same-model comparisons in our July 3 pull, recertified/renewed ran ~19-22% below new — the 8TB WD Red Plus at $275 renewed vs $339.99 new (~19%), and the 16TB IronWolf Pro at $499.99 recertified vs $640 new (~22%). Renewed/recertified listings showed up in every search we ran.

The warranty catch is also real. ServerPartDeals — a common recertified-drive seller — defines a “Manufacturer Recertified” drive in its FAQ as an item “tested, recertified, and approved by the original manufacturer… without a manufacturer warranty.” In other words, the original manufacturer warranty does not carry over. The coverage you actually get is the seller’s own, stated per product — one live recertified Exos listing shows a “2 Year Limited Period Warranty” plus a 30-day satisfaction return window. Warranty length is set per product, not by a single site-wide number, so read the specific listing.

Second signal to watch: review depth. The renewed 8TB WD Red Plus in our pull carried only 3 ratings versus 1,100 on the new version of the same model — a thin trust signal worth weighing.

Net: recertified is a smart value move if you run redundancy (RAID/RAIDZ so a single failure doesn’t lose data), keep a real 3-2-1 backup, and accept a seller warranty in place of the manufacturer’s. It is not the pick for someone running a single drive with no backup.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best size hard drive for a NAS?
For most home NAS builds in 2026, 8TB to 16TB per drive is the practical sweet spot. Smaller than 8TB rarely saves enough to matter, and beyond 16TB the price-per-terabyte does not fall reliably - in our July 3, 2026 Amazon pull a new 12TB WD Red Plus cost $48.53/TB while the new 8TB was cheaper at $42.50/TB, so bigger did not mean cheaper per terabyte. Buy CMR NAS-rated drives, size them so your array leaves room to grow one bay at a time, and remember that in RAID you lose capacity to redundancy (a parity or mirror drive), so a 4-bay NAS of 8TB drives does not give you 32TB usable.
Is it better to use SSD or HDD for a NAS?
For bulk NAS storage in 2026, HDDs are still the better buy, and it is not close on price. A new 8TB NAS HDD ran roughly $340-$445 in our July 3, 2026 pull ($42-$56/TB), while an SSD of the same capacity costs several times more per terabyte. SSDs make sense in a NAS as a cache tier or for a small, fast pool (VMs, databases, editing scratch), but for the multi-terabyte media and backup bulk that most NAS owners actually need, spinning CMR drives deliver far more capacity per dollar. Many builders run a hybrid: HDDs for bulk, one or two SSDs for cache.
Why not use SSD for a NAS?
Three reasons: cost per terabyte, capacity ceilings, and the fact that a NAS rarely needs SSD speed. Bulk SSD storage costs several times more per terabyte than a NAS HDD, and a spinning drive is not the bottleneck for a typical home NAS - a single mechanical drive already exceeds what 2.5GbE networking can carry. Unless your workload is random-access heavy (VMs, databases, many concurrent users), the network or the drive count limits you long before a single HDD’s speed does. SSDs earn their place as a cache in front of HDDs, not as the whole array.
What is CMR vs SMR, and does it matter for a NAS?
CMR (conventional magnetic recording) writes data tracks side by side; SMR (shingled magnetic recording) overlaps them like roof shingles to pack in more density. It matters a lot for a NAS: SMR’s device-managed rewrite behavior can drastically slow random writes and RAID rebuilds, which is why every current dedicated NAS-Pro drive family - WD Red Pro, Seagate IronWolf Pro, and Toshiba N300 - is 100% CMR per the manufacturer spec sheets. The one line to avoid for RAID is the legacy plain WD Red ‘EFAX’ drives (WD20/30/40/60EFAX), which Western Digital’s own product brief lists as SMR - the family at the center of the 2020 SMR-in-NAS backlash. Buy CMR for any NAS.
Who makes the best NAS hard drives, and which brand should I buy?
Three brands own the current NAS-drive market: Western Digital (WD Red Plus and WD Red Pro), Seagate (IronWolf and IronWolf Pro), and Toshiba (N300 and N300 Pro). There is no single ‘best brand’ - they trade blows on warranty and workload rating. WD Red Pro, IronWolf Pro, and Toshiba N300 Pro all carry a 5-year warranty; WD Red Plus, base IronWolf, and standard N300 carry 3 years. All current NAS-Pro families are CMR. Pick by tier and price for your capacity, not by brand loyalty.
Are recertified or refurbished NAS drives worth it?
They can cut roughly 19-22% off the same model’s new price - in our July 3, 2026 Amazon pull, a renewed 8TB WD Red Plus was $275 vs $339.99 new (~19% off) and a recertified 16TB IronWolf Pro was $499.99 vs $640 new (~22% off, and the cheapest per-TB drive in the whole comparison at $31.25/TB). The catch is warranty: a ‘Manufacturer Recertified’ drive does not carry the original manufacturer warranty - per ServerPartDeals’ own FAQ, the coverage you get is the seller’s, stated per product (one live recertified Exos listing showed a 2-year seller warranty). Recertified is a genuine value play if you run redundancy and back up, and you accept a thinner warranty.
How many years do NAS drives last?
There is no fixed lifespan, but the spec sheets give a useful proxy: current NAS drives are rated for MTBF/MTTF of roughly 1 to 2.5 million hours and workload limits of 180TB/year (WD Red Plus, Toshiba N300) up to 550TB/year (WD Red Pro, IronWolf Pro). Those are population statistics, not a guarantee for your specific drive. Plan for drives to fail - run RAID redundancy so one failure does not lose data, keep a 3-2-1 backup, and treat the warranty length (3 or 5 years) as the manufacturer’s own confidence signal.
