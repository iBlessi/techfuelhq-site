TL;DR · Which Seagate drive for your NAS

  • Light-duty home NAS (2–4 bays): standard IronWolf — 180TB/year is plenty, and it's one of the cheapest new NAS-rated CMR drives. Tops out at 16TB.
  • Heavy 24-7 use or a big array: IronWolf Pro — 550TB/year workload, 5-year warranty, 3 years of bundled Rescue Data Recovery, up to 32TB.
  • Best price-per-TB (the homelab move): a recertified enterprise Exos. A recertified 16TB Exos was $30.62/TB in our July 20 pull vs ~$43/TB for a new NAS drive — but check it's SATA, not SAS.
  • The reality: IronWolf Pro and Exos are the same base drive with different firmware, grade, and warranty. For most home NAS builds, buy on price and warranty channel, not the badge.
  • The one rule: whatever tier you pick, run redundancy and keep a real 3-2-1 backup. RAID is not a backup.

The short version

Seagate sells three families that all end up in NAS boxes, and the naming makes them sound like a simple good–better–best ladder. They mostly aren’t. Here is the 30-second version:

  • IronWolf is the home/SOHO NAS drive: CMR, 1-to-8-bay rated, capacities from 2TB to 16TB, a 180TB/year workload rating, and a 3-year warranty.
  • IronWolf Pro is the heavier-duty NAS drive: same CMR, but a 550TB/year workload, 2.5-million-hour MTBF on the big models, a 5-year warranty, and capacities all the way to 32TB.
  • Exos is the enterprise/datacenter drive that homelabbers buy for its price per terabyte — the same 7200-RPM CMR mechanics, rated to a 0.35% annual failure rate, but sold through channels where recertified units routinely undercut new NAS drives on $/TB.

The twist that the ranking forum threads and the AI Overview never quite pin down: IronWolf Pro and Exos are, on the spec sheet, essentially the same drive — and the current IronWolf Pro has quietly closed the reliability-rating gap that used to separate them. So the real decision is less “which is the better drive” and more “which capacity, at which price, through which warranty channel.” Let’s ground all of that in the actual data sheets and a dated price pull. For the box these drives go into, see our budget NAS build guide; for the cross-brand best-picks by use case (WD Red Plus, Toshiba N300, and where Seagate fits), see the best NAS hard drives guide. This page is specifically about choosing within the Seagate lineup.

Three things worth pulling out of that table:

  • The standard IronWolf now reaches 16TB. Seagate’s September 2025 IronWolf data sheet lists 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 16TB; the old 14TB and 18TB SKUs were dropped. If you see an 18TB “IronWolf” (non-Pro), it’s old stock, not the current line.
  • RV sensors are on every current model, even standard IronWolf. Older IronWolf data sheets marked the smallest capacities as lacking rotational-vibration sensors; the current sheet lists RV sensors as “yes” across the whole line. That’s a real improvement for anyone putting standard IronWolf into a multi-bay enclosure.
  • The workload gap is the clearest real difference. 180 vs 550 TB/year is the number that actually separates IronWolf from Pro/Exos. For a home NAS storing media and backups you will rarely approach even 180TB/year of writes; for a busy multi-user or surveillance array, the 550 rating (and the 5-year warranty that comes with it) is the honest reason to step up.

Are Exos and IronWolf Pro the same drive?

This is the single most-argued question in every Seagate NAS thread, and the forum answer — “they’re the same drive, Exos just has different firmware” — turns out to be mostly right, but nobody grounds it. Here’s the spec-sheet evidence.

Line up Seagate’s own data sheets for a matched 16TB Exos X16 and IronWolf Pro and the mechanical fingerprint is identical, down to the gram:

  • Weight: 670 g on both.
  • Dimensions: 26.11 × 101.85 × 146.99 mm on both.
  • Spindle / cache / latency: 7200 RPM, 256MB cache, 4.16 ms average latency — identical.
  • Read-error rate: 1 per 10^15 bits on both.
  • Vibration tolerance: 12.5 rad/s² on both.

That is not what two different drives look like. It’s what one base platform looks like, wearing two firmware loads and two labels. Seagate has never published a component-level bill of materials, so this is strong circumstantial evidence rather than a teardown — but a weight-to-the-gram, dimension-for-dimension, latency-for-latency match across two separate spec sheets is about as close as public documentation gets.

What actually differs is the grade and the software:

  • Validation / reliability rating. Exos is rated to a 0.35% AFR and 2.5M-hour MTBF; the older IronWolf Pro that shared the 16TB platform was rated 0.73% AFR / 1.2M-hour MTBF on its 2019 data sheet (linked in Sources). That’s an enterprise validation and binning difference, not a different mechanism.
  • Firmware and features. Exos adds enterprise-oriented features (PowerBalance, SuperParity, security/SED options, and a SAS variant). IronWolf Pro adds the consumer-friendly stack: IronWolf Health Management and 3 years of bundled Rescue Data Recovery.

Here’s the part the old threads miss: on the current data sheets, IronWolf Pro has caught up. Today’s IronWolf Pro (the “NT” models) is rated to the same 550 TB/year workload and the same 2.5-million-hour MTBF as Exos on the helium 12TB-and-up capacities. The historical “Exos is the more durable tier” gap is now largely a legacy-spec artifact — the current Pro is specced right alongside Exos, and simply adds Rescue and a retail warranty channel on top.

So the accurate way to say it: IronWolf Pro and Exos are the same base drive at similar grade; you’re really choosing a feature set and a purchase channel. Retail IronWolf Pro gets you Rescue, easy RMA, and a shelf full of new units. Exos gets you the enterprise feature options and — crucially — the recertified market, which is where the price story lives.

Two myths, spec-checked

Because Exos is an “enterprise” drive, two beliefs follow it around. Both fail against Seagate’s own numbers.

“Exos is louder.” At a matched generation, the Exos X-series and IronWolf Pro publish the same typical acoustics: about 2.8 bels idle and 3.2 bels seek. There is a genuine wrinkle worth flagging that no competitor mentions: Seagate reports acoustics inconsistently across its own lines — the consumer IronWolf sheet uses sound-pressure dBA, while the Pro and Exos sheets use sound-power bels — so a clean cross-line “X dBA vs Y dBA” comparison doesn’t actually exist in the source data. The honest read: all three are 7200-RPM drives, all are audible under seek, none is engineered to be quiet, and Exos’s louder reputation is mostly spin-up chatter plus the fact that people run bare enterprise drives in undampened cases. The homelab community consensus (r/DataHoarder, r/homelab) lands in the same place: recertified Exos “aren’t louder than IronWolf once spun up,” they’re just loud at boot — so isolate the NAS in a closet or a dampened case rather than choosing your drive by noise.

“Exos draws more power.” Also not supported at a matched size. At 16TB, idle power is identical (5.0W on both) the Exos X16 and IronWolf Pro. Their operating figures aren’t directly comparable because Seagate publishes “max operating” for Exos and “average operating” for IronWolf Pro — different definitions, not a real gap. The newer, denser Exos X24 does idle higher (about 6.3W) than the 2019-era 16TB drives, but that’s a generational change (bigger cache, denser platters), not an Exos-versus-IronWolf penalty.

Which one for your NAS

Sort by what your NAS actually is:

  • A quiet 2–4 bay home NAS for media and backups. Standard IronWolf is enough. You will not touch the 180TB/year ceiling, RV sensors are now standard across the line, and it’s among the cheapest new NAS-rated CMR drives. If a recertified Exos of the same size is meaningfully cheaper per TB and you run redundancy, take the Exos instead (see pricing below).
  • A busy always-on array, many users, virtualization, or 6+ bays. Step to IronWolf Pro (or Exos). The 550TB/year workload and 5-year warranty are the point, and the bundled 3-year Rescue is a genuine safety net. Between Pro and Exos here, it’s price and warranty channel: new-with-Rescue (Pro) versus cheapest-per-TB (recertified Exos).
  • Chasing maximum capacity per dollar and you run ZFS/parity + backups. Recertified enterprise Exos, full stop — it’s the homelab budget path and has been for years. Just confirm SATA vs SAS and treat the warranty as the seller’s, not Seagate’s.
  • Surveillance/NVR duty specifically. Neither of these three — that’s what SkyHawk is for — but if you’re mixing, an IronWolf/Pro handles light NVR loads fine.

Whatever you pick, size the array after redundancy, not before: a four-bay box of 16TB drives is not 64TB usable once you account for a parity or mirror drive. Run the real numbers in the NAS & RAID storage calculator before you buy.

What the real-world failure data actually says

Spec-sheet MTBF is a modeled number, not a promise. The closest thing to real-world data is Backblaze’s Drive Stats, which tracks hundreds of thousands of drives in production. Their full-year 2025 failure-rate analysis covered 344,196 drives (the set left after excluding boot drives and thinly-populated models) and put the fleet-wide annualized failure rate at 1.36% (down from 1.55% in 2024), with a lifetime AFR of 1.30%.

Two honest caveats that most articles quoting Backblaze skip:

  • Backblaze runs Exos-class enterprise drives, not consumer IronWolf. Every Seagate model in their 2025 tables is an enterprise “ST…NM…” drive. Consumer IronWolf (ST…VN…) and IronWolf Pro (ST…NE…/NT…) don’t appear in the fleet at all — Backblaze buys enterprise. So “Backblaze shows Seagate is reliable” is really a statement about Exos-class drives, which is genuinely reassuring for the recertified-Exos route, but tells you little about the consumer IronWolf line.
  • The specific per-model annual percentages live in an image table that isn’t machine-readable, so we won’t quote a number we can’t verify — a good rule for any stat. What is verifiable from Backblaze’s own notes: several Exos-class Seagate models had a standout-low year (the 16TB ST16000NM002J logged a single failure across 2025 and zero in Q4), while an aging 10TB Exos-class model was flagged as a high-failure outlier at end-of-life. The pattern — modern high-capacity enterprise drives failing rarely, worn-out old ones failing more — is exactly what you’d expect, and it’s the argument for buying recertified drives that still have plenty of rated life and pairing them with redundancy.

Bottom line on reliability: Seagate rates Exos to 0.35% AFR, the current IronWolf Pro to the same 2.5M-hour MTBF, and Backblaze’s real fleet of enterprise Seagate drives runs well under a couple of percent a year. None of that replaces a backup. Every tier here fails eventually; the drive tier matters far less than running RAID/RAIDZ so one failure doesn’t lose data and keeping a real 3-2-1 backup.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between Seagate IronWolf and IronWolf Pro?
The split is workload and warranty, not recording technology (both are CMR). Per Seagate’s current data sheets, the standard IronWolf is rated for a 180TB/year workload, a 1,000,000-hour MTBF, and a 3-year warranty, and it tops out at 16TB for 1- to 8-bay NAS enclosures. IronWolf Pro roughly triples the workload to 550TB/year, raises MTBF to 2,500,000 hours on the helium 12TB-and-up models, carries a 5-year warranty, and scales to 32TB with no stated bay limit. Both now include 3 years of Rescue Data Recovery. For a light-duty 2-to-4-bay home NAS the standard IronWolf is usually enough; step up to Pro for constant 24-7 duty, large arrays, or the longer warranty.
Are Seagate Exos and IronWolf Pro the same drive?
Nearly — they share a base mechanical platform but differ in firmware, validation grade, and features. At a matched 16TB, Seagate’s own Exos X16 and IronWolf Pro data sheets list an identical mechanical fingerprint: same 670-gram weight, same dimensions, 7200 RPM, 256MB cache, the same 1-per-10^15 read-error rate, the same 12.5 rad/s² vibration tolerance, and the same 4.16ms latency. What differs is the tuning and grade: Exos is validated to a higher reliability rating (0.35% AFR, 2.5M-hour MTBF) and adds enterprise features like PowerBalance, security/SED options, and a SAS variant, while IronWolf Pro adds consumer niceties like IronWolf Health Management and 3 years of bundled Rescue Data Recovery. Data sheets cannot prove an identical bill of materials, but the exact spec match is strong evidence of a shared platform with different firmware.
Are Exos drives good for a home NAS?
Yes, with two caveats. Exos drives are enterprise CMR disks that run fine in a home NAS, and they are usually the best price per terabyte, especially recertified. The first caveat is the interface: some Exos listings (and many recertified ones) are SAS, which will not connect to a standard SATA NAS bay without an HBA — confirm the model is SATA before buying. The second is that Exos has no Rescue Data Recovery and, if recertified, usually no original manufacturer warranty. For a homelab that runs redundancy (RAID/RAIDZ) and keeps a real backup, a recertified SATA Exos is the smart-money pick; for a single-drive setup with no backup, the retail warranty and Rescue of an IronWolf Pro is worth paying for.
Can I mix IronWolf, IronWolf Pro, and Exos in the same NAS?
Yes. There is no requirement that every drive in a NAS be the same model or even the same brand, and mixing IronWolf, IronWolf Pro, and Exos is common in homelabs. The practical rules: the drives in a given RAID group or ZFS vdev should be the same usable capacity (the array sizes to the smallest member), all should be CMR (they are, across the Seagate NAS and Exos lines), and it is good practice to keep drives within a vdev matched where you can, since your array runs at the pace and reliability floor of its weakest member. Mixing 7200-RPM enterprise drives with slower models is fine functionally but can add noise and heat.
Is Exos louder than IronWolf?
Not on Seagate’s published numbers. At a matched generation, both the Exos X-series and IronWolf Pro publish typical A-weighted acoustics of about 2.8 bels idle and 3.2 bels seek — the same figures. Seagate reports acoustics inconsistently across its own lines (the consumer IronWolf sheet uses sound-pressure dBA while the Pro and Exos sheets use sound-power bels), so a clean apples-to-apples number does not really exist. The honest takeaway: all three are 7200-RPM drives that are audible under seek, none is engineered to be quiet, and Exos’s ’louder’ reputation is mostly spin-up chatter and the fact that people run them in bare, undampened cases. If noise matters, isolate the NAS rather than pick the drive by it.
Which Seagate NAS drive is the best value in 2026?
For pure price-per-terabyte, a recertified enterprise Exos wins by a wide margin — in our July 20, 2026 Amazon snapshot a recertified 16TB Exos X16 was about $30.62/TB versus roughly $43/TB for a new IronWolf or IronWolf Pro of the same size, and larger recertified Exos dropped under $26/TB. That value comes with a thinner (seller, not Seagate) warranty and unknown prior hours, so it is a value play only if you run redundancy and back up. If you want new with a full 5-year warranty and bundled data recovery, IronWolf Pro is the pick; if you just need a cheap, light-duty new drive for a small home box, the standard IronWolf is enough.
