Horizontal versus vertical FOV

A single FOV number is ambiguous unless you know its axis. Horizontal FOV measures the angle across the screen’s width, vertical FOV across its height, and for the same scene they are linked by the aspect ratio: the wider the screen, the larger the horizontal figure is relative to the vertical. Many shooters expose horizontal FOV; a lot of newer and console-first engines expose vertical, sometimes labeled just “FOV.” That mismatch is the single most common reason a converted number does not match in-game, so if it looks off, switch the type and compare again.

Hor+ scaling and ultrawide

Most modern PC games use Hor+ (horizontal-plus) scaling: they hold the vertical field of view fixed and extend the horizontal to fill a wider screen. That is why a well-behaved game on an ultrawide shows more of the world at the sides rather than a stretched or zoomed image. The opposite, Vert- scaling, crops the top and bottom instead, which is what you get in games that do not properly support ultrawide. This tool uses Hor+, so converting a 16:9 setting to 21:9 holds the vertical view steady and widens the horizontal, matching how a correct game behaves.

What FOV to actually use

There is no single right value, but some anchors help. On 16:9, many players land between 90 and 105 degrees horizontal: higher feels more aware and is popular in competitive shooters, lower feels more cinematic. If you get motion sickness, a higher FOV usually helps by reducing the zoomed-in, swimming feel. Sitting closer to a bigger screen also argues for more FOV. On ultrawide, keep your vertical comfort level and let the horizontal number rise, which this tool does for you. Try a value, play a session, adjust. To confirm your screen’s exact ratio first, use the Aspect Ratio Calculator.

Assumptions and sources

  • The conversion is exact trigonometry, not fitted data: horizontal and vertical FOV are linked through the aspect ratio by tan(H/2) = ratio x tan(V/2), and the Hor+ conversion holds the vertical angle constant while re-solving the horizontal one.
  • Hor+ and Vert- scaling behavior, and the fact that games disagree about which axis their FOV slider uses, are documented on PCGamingWiki’s field-of-view page.
  • The 90-105 degree comfort range on 16:9 is a TechFuelHQ editorial guideline reflecting common shooter defaults, not a measured standard - it is stated here so you treat it as a starting point rather than a rule.

Frequently asked questions

What is field of view (FOV) in games?
Field of view is how much of the game world you see on screen at once, measured as an angle in degrees. A higher FOV shows more of the scene around you, which feels more open and helps you spot things at the edges, at the cost of making distant objects look smaller. A lower FOV zooms in, making the center larger but the view more tunnel-like. FOV is usually given as a horizontal or vertical angle, and the right number depends on your monitor’s aspect ratio, how close you sit, and personal comfort.
What is the difference between horizontal and vertical FOV?
Horizontal FOV measures the angle across the width of the screen; vertical FOV measures it across the height. For the same scene they are linked by the aspect ratio: the wider the screen, the larger the horizontal FOV is relative to the vertical. That is why a single FOV number is ambiguous unless you know which axis it refers to. Many shooters expose horizontal FOV, while a lot of newer engines and console-first games expose vertical FOV, sometimes just labeled ‘FOV.’ This tool shows both for any setting, so you can enter whichever your game uses and read off the other.
What is Hor+ scaling and why does it matter for ultrawide?
Hor+ (horizontal-plus) scaling keeps the vertical field of view fixed and extends the horizontal field of view to fill a wider screen. It is what most modern PC games use, and it is the reason an ultrawide monitor shows more of the world at the sides rather than a stretched or zoomed image. The opposite approach, Vert- scaling, would crop the top and bottom instead, which is what happens in games that do not properly support ultrawide. This calculator uses Hor+, so when you convert a 16:9 setting to 21:9 it holds the vertical view steady and widens the horizontal view, matching how a well-behaved game behaves.
What FOV should I use?
There is no single correct value, but some guidance helps. On 16:9, many players land between 90 and 105 degrees horizontal: higher feels more aware and is popular in competitive shooters, lower feels more cinematic and immersive. If you get motion sickness, a higher FOV often helps because it reduces the zoomed-in, swimming sensation. Sitting closer to a larger screen also argues for a higher FOV. On ultrawide, you generally keep the same vertical comfort level and let the horizontal number rise, which this tool does for you. Try a value, play for a session, and adjust.
How do I convert my FOV for an ultrawide monitor?
Enter your current FOV, pick whether it is horizontal or vertical, set your current aspect ratio (usually 16:9), then choose your ultrawide target such as 21:9 or 32:9. The tool holds the vertical view constant and gives the equivalent horizontal FOV for the wider screen, plus the vertical value in case your game uses that slider. In practice this means your game shows more at the sides on the ultrawide while keeping the same sense of zoom, which is the natural, non-stretched result you want.
Why doesn't the converted number match my game?
Almost always because the game uses a different FOV axis than you assumed. If you enter a horizontal value but the game’s slider is actually vertical, the numbers will not line up. Switch the type in the tool and compare again. A few older games also tie their FOV slider to a 4:3 base regardless of your real aspect ratio, so their slider number is not a true horizontal or vertical angle; for those, match the on-screen feel rather than the raw number. The geometry here is exact, so a mismatch points to how the game labels its setting, not the math.
