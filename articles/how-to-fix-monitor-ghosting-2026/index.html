The most common advice for monitor ghosting — “buy a new cable” — is usually wrong. Ghosting is not a cable problem. It’s a pixel response-time problem happening inside the panel, and the real fix is one setting most people either never touch or turn up too far. This guide explains what ghosting actually is, how to find your monitor’s overdrive sweet spot without creating the worse problem (inverse ghosting), and which kinds of ghosting genuinely can’t be fixed.

What monitor ghosting actually is

Ghosting is a faint trail or duplicate that follows moving objects across the screen. It happens because the monitor’s pixels change color too slowly: the next frame arrives before the previous pixel transition has finished, so you briefly see the old image fading behind the new one.

The spec that governs this is gray-to-gray (GtG) response time — how long a pixel takes to change from one shade to another, measured in milliseconds. Lower is better. Ghosting shows up whenever GtG is slower than the time between frames. And here’s the part that trips people up: refresh rate and response time are different things. A 144Hz monitor refreshes every 6.9ms and a 240Hz one every 4.2ms, but if the panel’s pixels take longer than that to switch, you’ll still get trails. A high refresh rate does not guarantee fast pixels.

The quickest way to confirm you actually have ghosting is the Blur Busters UFO test — the same free tool monitor reviewers use. A UFO slides across the screen; a faint UFO trailing behind it is ghosting.

Fix #1: Set overdrive correctly (the sweet spot, not the max)

This is the fix that solves most ghosting, and it’s already built into your monitor. Overdrive applies extra voltage to force pixels to switch faster, shrinking the response time so transitions fit inside the frame window. Depending on the brand, it’s in your monitor’s on-screen display (OSD) menu under one of these names:

  • Overdrive (generic)
  • Response Time (Samsung, many others)
  • OD (short form)
  • Trace Free (ASUS)
  • Overshoot / AMA (some BenQ/Acer)

The mistake almost everyone makes is setting it to maximum. Too much overdrive causes inverse ghosting (overshoot): instead of lagging behind the target shade, the pixel overshoots it and settles back, leaving a bright halo, a dark outline, or a purple “corona” around moving objects. That looks worse than the ghosting you started with.

How to find your sweet spot:

  1. Open the testufo.com UFO test in your browser at your monitor’s native refresh rate.
  2. Open your monitor’s OSD and find the overdrive setting.
  3. Cycle through the levels one at a time — Off → Low/Normal → Medium → High — watching the UFO after each change.
  4. Pick the highest level that still has clean trails with no bright/dark overshoot halo leading or following the UFO.

On most monitors the correct answer is the middle setting, not the top one. The maximum is there for marketing, not for you.

Fix #2: Run your native refresh rate

If your monitor isn’t actually running at its advertised refresh rate, motion looks worse and ghosting is more noticeable. Windows often defaults a new display to 60Hz.

Go to Settings → System → Display → Advanced display, and set Choose a refresh rate to the highest value your monitor supports. If your top refresh rate isn’t listed, you’re likely bandwidth-limited by your cable or port (more on that below). Running at native refresh gives the panel the shortest frame window and lets a correct overdrive setting do its job. This is also the foundation for fixing screen tearing, which is a related but separate motion problem.

Fix #3: Rule out in-game motion blur (it’s not really ghosting)

If you only see “ghosting” inside games and not on your desktop, it may not be your monitor at all. Many games ship with a Motion Blur effect enabled on purpose, and it’s easy to mistake for panel ghosting. Open the game’s video/graphics settings and turn Motion Blur off (and Film Grain while you’re there). If the trailing disappears, it was a software effect, not your hardware — no monitor tweaking needed.

Fix #4: Know your panel type (VA “black smear” can’t be fully fixed)

Not all ghosting is fixable, and it’s important to be honest about that. If you have a VA (Vertical Alignment) panel, you’ll likely see black smearing — a dark trailing smear behind objects moving over dark backgrounds. This is an inherent characteristic of how VA pixels transition in dark scenes, and overdrive only partly helps. You can lessen it by nudging brightness and gamma up slightly in the OSD, but you can’t eliminate it the way you can on an IPS or OLED panel.

If dark-scene smearing genuinely bothers you and you game in a dark room, the fix isn’t a setting — it’s a different panel technology. IPS ghosts far less, and OLED effectively has none. If you’re weighing an upgrade, our guide on using an OLED as a PC monitor covers the motion-clarity trade-offs, and the 1440p vs 4K gaming breakdown helps you match the panel to the GPU you actually have.

The cable question — answered honestly

You’ll see “replace your cable” as the top fix everywhere. For true ghosting, a cable won’t help — ghosting is a response-time problem inside the panel, full stop.

Where a cable does matter is a different symptom that gets confused with ghosting: signal artifacts. A failing, damaged, or under-spec cable can cause random sparkles, brief black flashes, or your monitor silently dropping to a lower refresh rate — and a monitor stuck at 60Hz when it should run 144Hz will look far worse in motion. If the artifact is instead steady vertical or horizontal lines on the screen, the monitor’s own OSD menu settles the cable-versus-panel question in one press. If you see those symptoms, or your native refresh rate won’t appear in Windows, then ruling out the cable is worthwhile: a properly rated VESA-certified DisplayPort cable or an Ultra High Speed HDMI 2.1 cable guarantees the bandwidth for high-refresh signals. But do this to fix signal artifacts or a capped refresh rate, not trailing-behind-motion ghosting — for that, go back to overdrive.

The fix ladder

In order, this is how to kill monitor ghosting:

  1. Confirm it’s ghosting with the testufo.com UFO test (trailing = ghosting; leading halo = too much overdrive).
  2. Set overdrive to the sweet spot — the highest level with no overshoot halo, usually the middle option.
  3. Run your native refresh rate in Windows display settings.
  4. Turn off in-game Motion Blur if you only see it in games.
  5. Accept VA black smear as inherent, or move to IPS/OLED if it bothers you.
  6. Only then consider the cable, and only if you also have signal artifacts or a capped refresh rate.

Most people fix ghosting entirely at step 2. It’s a five-minute setting, not a hardware purchase — which is exactly why “buy a new cable” is such bad first advice.

Frequently asked questions

Is monitor ghosting permanent?
No. Ghosting is a real-time motion artifact, not permanent damage — it’s completely different from OLED burn-in or a dead pixel. It only appears while something is moving on screen, because it’s caused by pixels changing color too slowly. Adjust your monitor’s overdrive/response-time setting, run it at its native refresh rate, and the ghosting reduces or disappears. Nothing is physically wrong with the panel; it’s a settings-and-response-time issue, with the exception of inherent VA-panel dark smearing.
Why is my 144Hz (or 240Hz) monitor still ghosting?
Because a high refresh rate does not guarantee fast pixels. A 144Hz monitor draws a new frame every 6.9ms and a 240Hz panel every 4.2ms, but if the panel’s gray-to-gray (GtG) pixel transition takes longer than that frame window, the previous frame is still visibly fading when the next one arrives — that trailing smear is ghosting. Refresh rate and pixel response time are two different specs. The fix is your monitor’s overdrive setting, which speeds up the pixel transitions to fit inside the frame window.
What overdrive setting should I use?
The sweet spot, not the maximum. Overdrive (also labeled Response Time, OD, Trace Free, or Overshoot depending on the brand) applies extra voltage to make pixels switch faster. But turning it to the highest setting usually causes inverse ghosting — the pixel overshoots its target and leaves a pale halo or dark ‘corona’ around moving objects, which looks worse than the original ghosting. Cycle through the levels (Off, Low/Normal, Medium, High) while watching a motion test, and pick the highest one that has clean trails with no bright overshoot halo. On most monitors that’s the middle setting.
Does a bad cable cause monitor ghosting?
Not true ghosting. Real ghosting is a pixel response-time problem inside the panel, so a new cable won’t fix it. What a failing or under-spec cable can cause is signal artifacts — random sparkles, black flashes, or the monitor silently dropping to a lower refresh rate — which can look similar to ghosting and are easy to confuse with it. If you see those symptoms, or your monitor won’t hold its advertised refresh rate, a properly rated DisplayPort or HDMI cable is worth ruling out. If your only symptom is trailing behind motion, it’s response time, not the cable.
How do I test my monitor for ghosting?
Use the Blur Busters UFO test at testufo.com — it moves a UFO across the screen at a set speed. If a faint duplicate trails behind it, that’s ghosting; if a bright or dark outline leads or follows it, that’s inverse ghosting from too-much overdrive. Run the test while you cycle your overdrive levels to find the cleanest setting for your panel. It’s the same tool reviewers use, it’s free, and it takes about a minute.
Can VA panel black smearing be fixed?
Not completely — it’s an inherent limitation of VA (Vertical Alignment) panels. VA pixels transition slowly in dark scenes, so you get a dark trailing smear behind moving objects on black or near-black backgrounds, even with overdrive on. You can lessen it by nudging the monitor’s brightness or gamma up slightly and by using overdrive, but you can’t eliminate it the way you can on an IPS or OLED panel. If dark-scene smearing bothers you and you game in dark rooms, the real fix is a different panel type, not a setting.
