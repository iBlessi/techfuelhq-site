The 2026 baseline, and why it moved

For years the answer to “how much RAM do I need” was 16GB, and it was a comfortable answer. It stopped being comfortable in 2026 for three reasons that have nothing to do with raw frame rate:

  • Windows got hungrier. A clean Windows 11 install idles at roughly 3 to 4GB with nothing open, and in normal use - a browser, Discord, a few tray apps - the OS and background overhead commonly sit around 4 to 6GB before you launch a game.
  • Game engines got hungrier. Modern titles across every big engine now reserve well over 16GB at high settings. Testing outlets through 2026 have measured demanding releases such as Indiana Jones and the Great Circle (id Tech 7) and Dragon Age: The Veilguard (Frostbite) using comfortably past 16GB of system RAM at high settings and 1440p — and Unreal Engine 5 titles trend the same way.
  • The messaging caught up. In 2026 Microsoft published a Windows gaming support doc calling 16GB the “practical starting point” and 32GB the “no-worries” upgrade for anyone running Discord, a browser, or a stream alongside their games - then removed it within days after backlash that pushing 32GB during a price spike was tone-deaf (Windows Latest). The recommendation was sound; the timing collided with the shortage.

So the baseline moved to 32GB not because games suddenly need double the frame-rate memory, but because the headroom math changed: subtract 4-6GB for the OS and 12-20GB for a modern game, and a 16GB machine is out of room while a 32GB machine still has slack. It is easier to see than to describe.

Is 32GB of RAM enough?

For almost everyone building or upgrading in 2026, yes. Gaming, everyday work, and heavy multitasking all sit comfortably inside 32GB, and it is the tier worth paying for even with the shortage inflating prices. The reason is headroom, not raw speed: after Windows 11 and background apps take their 4-6GB and a modern game reserves 12-20GB, a 32GB machine still has slack for Discord, a browser, and a stream at the same time - the exact load that pushes 16GB into swapping.

It also holds up over time. Because 16GB became the floor game developers design around, system-RAM demand from new titles has crept up slowly rather than doubling, so 32GB should stay comfortable for gaming for years. What actually pushes people past 32GB is not newer games - it is a change in workload: running several virtual machines, editing 4K video or large photo catalogs, or loading local AI models into system RAM. Those are the cases where 32GB stops being enough and 64GB earns its price (covered in the next section). If none of them describe you, 32GB is the ceiling worth buying - and the money above it is better spent on the GPU or a faster CPU.

Is 16GB enough for gaming in 2026?

It depends on what else is running. For pure gaming at 1080p in esports titles and older games, with a clean background, 16GB still plays fine. Two things break it:

  1. Modern AAA, especially Unreal Engine 5. These engines reserve more system RAM than older ones. On 16GB the system fills up and starts swapping to the SSD, which shows up as texture pop-in, micro-stutter, and “alt-tab lag” - not as a lower average FPS.
  2. Multitasking. Discord, a browser with tabs, a second monitor with a YouTube video, or a stream - each takes a bite, and on 16GB the game is competing for what is left.

This is the key nuance the benchmark headlines miss: 16GB and 32GB post similar average FPS in most games, but 32GB has clearly better 1% and 0.1% lows - the frame-time consistency you actually feel. If you want to see how your specific CPU and GPU balance out at your resolution, the PC Bottleneck Calculator shows which part limits you first; RAM headroom is the quiet third factor underneath it.

Bottom line: 16GB is enough to start, and it is a defensible budget choice in the shortage. It is not the comfortable choice for a new 2026 build.

32GB vs 64GB: where more stops helping

For gaming, 64GB shows essentially no benefit over 32GB - the extra capacity sits idle. 64GB earns its price only for workloads that genuinely reserve more than 32GB:

  • Running several virtual machines at once (a Proxmox or homelab workstation).
  • Video editing 4K timelines, or large Lightroom / photo catalogs.
  • Compiling large codebases.
  • Loading local AI models into system RAM when they do not fit in GPU VRAM.

If none of those describe you, 32GB is the ceiling worth paying for. And in 2026 specifically, 64GB kits carry a disproportionate shortage premium and are frequently out of stock, so “buy 64GB to be safe” is the expensive mistake this year - buy it for a real workload, not a hunch.

The part nobody wants to talk about: the price

Sizing RAM in 2026 is really a budgeting decision, because a DRAM shortage - the same one raising hard-drive and SSD prices - has pushed memory to roughly three to four times its 2024-2025 lows. A 32GB DDR5-6000 kit that ran around $95-130 in early 2026 now sits closer to $420-480, and 64GB kits are scarcer and pricier still (verified pricing and specific kit picks are in the DDR5 RAM buying guide).

That changes the advice in two practical ways:

  • Do not over-buy. The old “just get 64GB for future-proofing” logic doesn’t hold when 64GB costs what a GPU used to. Buy the tier your workload actually uses.
  • A staged 16-to-32 plan is legitimate. On a tight budget, a single 16GB stick now and a matching second stick later is a reasonable way to spread the cost - as long as you buy a board with a free slot and a kit you can match. (One caveat on AMD: four DIMMs can drop your stable memory speed, so prefer a 2x16GB kit over 4x8GB when you get to 32GB.)

If you are speccing a whole system around your RAM budget, the 2026 gaming PC build guide and the PC Builder balance the memory decision against the CPU and GPU so you don’t starve the parts that move frame rates most.

The verdict

  • Everyday / office use: 16GB is comfortable.
  • New gaming build in 2026: 32GB. It is the baseline for a reason - headroom for the OS, background apps, and modern engines, with far smoother 1% lows than 16GB.
  • Tight budget: 16GB now is defensible in the shortage; plan a matching second kit later and leave a slot open.
  • VMs, heavy content creation, local AI: 64GB, and only 96-128GB if a real workload pins it.
  • Gaming and nothing else: 64GB is overkill - the money is better spent on the GPU or a faster CPU.

The 2026 twist is cost, not capability. RAM is expensive right now, so the win is buying exactly what your use case needs - which for most people building to game is 32GB, and for most people upgrading on a budget is a smart 16GB-to-32GB path - and putting the savings where you will actually feel them.

Frequently asked questions

How much RAM do I need in 2026?
For a new PC in 2026, 32GB is the sensible baseline for gaming and general use, 16GB is the budget floor that still works for lighter workloads, and 64GB is for virtual machines, heavy content creation, or local AI. The reason 32GB has become the default is not raw frame rate - it is headroom. Windows 11 itself uses several gigabytes at idle, a browser with a dozen tabs plus Discord eats several more, and modern game engines increasingly reserve well over 16GB, so a 16GB machine spends its day near full and swaps to the SSD, which causes stutter. The honest complication in 2026 is price: a DRAM shortage has pushed RAM to roughly three to four times its 2024-2025 lows, so the goal is to buy the right amount for your actual use, not the most you can afford.
Is 16GB of RAM enough for gaming in 2026?
For pure gaming at 1080p in esports and older titles, with little running in the background, 16GB is still playable. Where it breaks down is modern AAA games - especially Unreal Engine 5 titles - and multitasking. Testing through 2026 shows 16GB and 32GB post similar average frame rates in many games, but the 1% and 0.1% lows (the stutters you actually feel) are noticeably better on 32GB, because a 16GB system fills its memory and starts swapping to storage. If you run Discord, a browser, or a stream alongside the game, 16GB is the part you will feel first. It is enough to start; it is not the comfortable choice for a new build in 2026.
16GB vs 32GB RAM - is 32GB worth it?
For a new gaming or work PC, yes. The average FPS difference between 16GB and 32GB is small in most games - often under 5% - so if you only look at average frame rate, 16GB looks fine. But 32GB smooths out the 1% lows, absorbs background apps, and gives modern engines the headroom they now expect, which is why Microsoft, PC Gamer, and the build community all shifted to calling 32GB the 2026 baseline. The one real counterargument in 2026 is cost: the RAM shortage widened the price gap, so on a tight budget, 16GB now and a second matching kit later is a legitimate plan - just buy a kit that leaves a slot open.
Do you need 64GB of RAM?
Most people do not. 64GB earns its place for specific workloads: running several virtual machines, editing high-resolution video or large Lightroom catalogs, compiling large codebases, or loading local AI models into system RAM. For gaming alone, 64GB shows essentially no benefit over 32GB today - the extra capacity sits unused. During the 2026 shortage, 64GB kits are also disproportionately expensive and often out of stock, so buy 64GB only if you have a workload that genuinely pins more than 32GB, not for future-proofing on a hunch.
How much RAM does Windows 11 use?
A clean Windows 11 install idles at roughly 3 to 4GB of RAM with nothing open, because it runs background services for security, search indexing, and updates. In real use - a browser with tabs, Discord, a couple of tray apps - the operating system and background overhead commonly sit around 4 to 6GB before you launch anything demanding. That baseline is exactly why 16GB feels tighter than the number suggests: subtract the OS overhead and a modern game reserving 12 to 20GB, and a 16GB machine is out of headroom, while a 32GB machine still has room to breathe.
Is 32GB overkill for gaming in 2026?
No - in 2026 it is the mainstream recommendation, not overkill. It was arguably overkill in 2020, when 16GB comfortably covered games and the OS. What changed is that game engines, background apps, and Windows itself all grew their memory appetite, and the price gap between 16GB and 32GB narrowed for years before the shortage. 64GB is where ‘overkill for gaming’ begins today. If your machine only ever games and never multitasks, 16GB still works - but 32GB is the amount that keeps a 2026 build smooth without paying for capacity you cannot use.
Is 32GB of RAM enough?
For the overwhelming majority of 2026 builds - gaming, everyday work, and heavy multitasking - yes, 32GB is enough, and it is the amount worth paying for even during the shortage. The math is headroom: subtract the 4 to 6GB Windows 11 and background apps use, and the 12 to 20GB a modern game reserves, and 32GB still leaves real room for Discord, a browser, and a stream running alongside - the exact combination that fills a 16GB machine and forces it to swap to the SSD. The situations where 32GB is not enough are specific and non-gaming: running several virtual machines, editing 4K video or large photo catalogs, or loading local AI models into system RAM. If none of those describe you, 32GB is the ceiling worth buying.
Is 32GB of RAM enough for the next 5 years?
For gaming and general use, 32GB should stay comfortable for years. The reason is that 16GB became the floor that game developers target, so system-RAM demand from new games has grown slowly rather than doubling the way it did last decade. The thing that would push you past 32GB is not newer games - it is a change in what you do with the PC, such as adding virtual machines, 4K video editing, or running local AI models, which reserve memory that gaming never touches. Buy 32GB for a typical gaming or work build and it has years of headroom; only size up to 64GB when a specific heavy workload genuinely pins more than 32GB.
Is 32GB of RAM enough for video editing?
For 1080p and 1440p editing, 32GB is comfortable and handles most timelines and effects without swapping. Where it starts to matter is 4K timelines, large Lightroom or photo catalogs, and long projects with many layers or high-resolution assets - those benefit from 64GB, because editors cache more of the project in RAM as the resolution and catalog size climb. If your editing is occasional or stays at 1080p/1440p, 32GB is enough; if 4K video or big catalogs are your daily work, 64GB is the tier that stops the editor from spilling to disk.
