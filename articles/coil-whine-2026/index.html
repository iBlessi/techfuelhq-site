You fire up a game, the frame counter shoots past 300 in the menu, and your PC starts singing — a high-pitched electronic zing that rises and falls with the action on screen. Search the noise and the internet splits into the same two camps every time: that’s coil whine, it’s totally normal, ignore it — or return that card immediately. Both answers skip the two questions that actually matter, and that’s why people either RMA a perfectly good card or spend weeks ignoring a genuine warning sign.

The two questions worth answering, in order, are: is it even coil whine? and where is it coming from? Get those right and everything else — whether to worry, whether to swap it, how to quiet it down — falls out cleanly. Here’s the diagnostic the “it’s normal, move on” articles skip.

Before the steps, the single most useful reframe — because the whole internet argument is really about this one axis. Almost the entire range of “my PC is making a noise” is harmless. A thin sliver at the far end isn’t. Here’s where the sounds actually fall:

Step 1: Is it actually coil whine?

Coil whine has a signature, and it’s not the sound — it’s the behavior. The noise itself is a high-pitched buzzing, squealing, chirping, or zinging; sharp and electronic rather than mechanical. But lots of PC noises are high-pitched. What sets coil whine apart is that it changes with electrical load, not with temperature or fan speed.

Run this quick sort before you assume anything:

What you hearWhat it tracksLikely source
High-pitched zing/buzz, pitch changes with frame rate (spikes in menus, shifts when you alt-tab)Electrical loadCoil whine
Whoosh or hum that rises smoothly with loadFan RPM / temperatureA cooling fan
Grinding, rattling, or clickingWorn bearingA failing fan — see the fan-bearing test
Gurgling or bubblingCoolant/air in the loopAIO pump
Sharp crackle or pop, maybe a burning smellArcing — dangerPower off and unplug now

The single most reliable tell: does the pitch change when the frame rate changes? Open a game menu (frame rates there are often absurdly high — hundreds or thousands of fps with nothing to render), then load into the game. If the whine spikes in the menu and settles in-game, or shifts when you alt-tab and scroll, it’s coil whine. If instead the noise climbs with temperature and fan speed, you’re chasing a fan, not a coil — and a grinding one is a worn bearing you should deal with.

That last row matters most. Coil whine is harmless, but a crackle-pop with a burning smell is arcing electricity, and that’s the one PC noise you never wait out.

Step 2: Where is it coming from — GPU, PSU, or motherboard?

In a desktop, coil whine nearly always comes from one of three places, in this order of likelihood: the GPU, then the PSU, then the motherboard’s VRM. Knowing which one changes what you do about it — and which component you’d swap.

Use load type to narrow it down:

  • GPU — tracks frame rate and GPU load. It’s loudest in high-fps scenes, menus, and loading screens. This is by far the most common source, especially on modern high-wattage cards.
  • PSU — tracks total system power draw. It tends to show up under heavy combined CPU-and-GPU load, or at particular power states, rather than following your frame counter specifically.
  • Motherboard VRM — rarest, comes from around the CPU socket, and follows CPU load and voltage transitions.

The fastest GPU test: set your monitor to 60 Hz and turn on V-Sync, or cap your FPS in the driver. If the whine drops or vanishes, it’s frame-rate-driven and the GPU is your culprit. That one test resolves the majority of cases — and it looks like this:

To localize it physically, roll a sheet of A4 paper into a tube (or use a paper-towel roll), hold one end to your ear, and point the other at each component in turn — GPU, then PSU, then the VRM area above the CPU socket. The tube blocks surrounding noise and helps you home in. One big caveat: the coils sit on a circuit board, and the board acts like a speaker cone — so the whine resonates through the whole case and can genuinely sound like it’s coming from the wrong place. Don’t fully trust your ears; confirm with the load-type behavior above.

When you truly can’t tell, the definitive method is swapping. Start with the GPU (easiest and most likely) — drop in another card and see if the noise goes with it. If it persists, try a different PSU. The motherboard is the last and hardest swap, so it’s a diagnosis of exclusion: if the whine survives both a GPU and PSU swap, the board’s VRM is what’s singing.

On a laptop, the rules bend a little. You can’t swap the GPU or PSU, so localization matters less — but laptops have one tell desktops don’t: the whine often changes when you plug or unplug the charger. Because the charging circuit and the discharge-from-battery path deliver power differently, a laptop can whine only on AC, only on battery, or shift pitch between the two. If yours does that, it’s classic coil whine from the power delivery, not a fault. The fixes are the same load-smoothing ones — cap the frame rate first, then undervolt with your vendor’s utility (or a global FPS limit) — and since you can’t swap parts, the retailer return window is your main lever if it’s truly intolerable.

Step 3: Is it normal, or the one warning sign?

Here’s the reassuring part, and it’s well established: coil whine does not damage your hardware. It doesn’t hurt performance, it doesn’t shorten the component’s lifespan, and it isn’t a symptom of anything failing. It’s a physical quirk of how inductors behave under fluctuating current — annoying, harmless, and extremely common on powerful modern GPUs. If you’ve confirmed steady GPU coil whine that’s always been there and tracks your frame rate, you can genuinely stop worrying about the hardware and decide purely on how much the noise bothers you.

The exceptions are specific and worth memorizing:

  • New or worsening PSU buzz paired with crashes. Occasional light coil whine from a power supply is normal, but a buzz that’s new, getting louder, and accompanied by random shutdowns, BSODs, or instability under load can be a power supply on its way out — not benign coil whine. That combination is worth taking seriously: the signs of a failing PSU walk through how to confirm it’s the power supply and not a dying GPU or bad RAM.
  • A sharp crackle, pop, or sizzle — especially with a burning or electrical smell. This is not coil whine. It’s arcing or a component letting go. Shut the PC off and unplug it from the wall immediately; don’t power it back on to “check.”

Everything else — the steady zing that rises with your frame counter — is the harmless kind. The question becomes how to quiet it, not whether to panic.

Step 4: How to actually reduce it (and what to never do)

The fixes that genuinely work all do the same thing: reduce or smooth the electrical load hitting the coils. In rough order of effort-to-payoff:

  1. Cap your frame rate. This is the big one. Uncapped frame rates — 900 fps in a menu, 400 in an old game — are the most common trigger, because the card is thrashing its power delivery to render frames you’ll never see. Turn on V-Sync, enable G-Sync/FreeSync, or set an FPS limit in the NVIDIA or AMD driver to match your monitor. Many people’s whine disappears entirely right here.
  2. Undervolt the GPU. In MSI Afterburner (works on any brand), flatten the voltage/frequency curve so the card holds its clocks at a lower voltage. Less voltage means less current through the inductors and often less whine — and it lowers temperatures with little or no performance loss as a bonus. It’s the same trick that helps with GPU overheating, so it’s worth learning either way.
  3. Consider the power supply. A cheap or aging PSU with sloppy voltage regulation can feed “dirty” power that makes whine worse across the whole system. A quality unit with tight regulation can help — though this is a bigger spend, so try the free fixes first.
  4. Mechanical damping, carefully. Moving the PC further from your ears, closing a mesh case panel, or standing the tower on a soft surface can mask the sound. Some enthusiasts very carefully add non-conductive damping — but this is fiddly and easy to get wrong.

What to never do: do not apply superglue, hot glue, or nail polish directly to the coils. It’s a widely repeated bad tip — those adhesives wick under surrounding components, trap heat against parts designed to shed it, make any future repair impossible, and void the warranty; and anything actually conductive risks a dead short on top of that. BIOS clock tweaks by themselves also won’t touch coil whine — it’s a hardware characteristic, not a software setting (undervolting works because it changes power draw, not because it’s a “setting”).

The RMA reality

Almost every manufacturer treats coil whine as a normal characteristic, not a defect — so a warranty RMA for coil whine alone usually gets rejected. Your real lever is the retailer’s return window. Because whine varies from unit to unit even within the same exact model, swapping a whiny card for another copy inside a no-questions-asked return period (Amazon, Best Buy, Micro Center) is a legitimate gamble — the next one may be dead silent. Outside that window, a warranty claim generally only lands if the whine comes bundled with a real fault like crashing or artifacting. If you’re on a brand-new part and the whine is mild, it’s also reasonable to just live with it for a few weeks first — sometimes it settles down on its own.

The quick version

  1. Confirm it’s coil whine, not a fan or arcing. Coil whine’s pitch changes with frame rate; fan noise tracks temperature and RPM (grinding = worn bearing); a crackle-pop with a burning smell is arcing — power off now.
  2. Find the source by load type. Follows FPS → GPU (set 60 Hz + V-Sync to confirm). Follows total power draw → PSU. Around the CPU socket, follows CPU load → motherboard VRM. Use a paper tube to localize, but remember the case resonates.
  3. Decide if it matters. Steady GPU whine that tracks frame rate is harmless — it won’t damage anything. New PSU buzz with crashes is a different story: check for PSU failure.
  4. Reduce it the right way. Cap FPS (V-Sync/G-Sync/FreeSync), undervolt in Afterburner, quality PSU. Never glue the coils. RMA rarely works for whine alone — use the retailer’s return window and roll the dice on another unit.

Sort is it coil whine and where from before you do anything, and you’ll stop returning cards that were fine — and stop tuning out a power supply that was actually trying to warn you.

Frequently asked questions

Is coil whine bad or harmful to my hardware?
In almost every case, no. Coil whine is purely an acoustic side effect — an inductor (a coil of wire) vibrating at an audible frequency as current fluctuates through it. It does not damage the component, hurt performance, or shorten its lifespan; it is a nuisance, not a fault. There is one exception worth knowing: if a whine or buzz is brand-new, getting worse, and paired with random crashes or shutdowns under load, that can point at a failing power supply rather than benign coil whine. And a sharp electrical crackle or pop — especially with a burning smell — is not coil whine at all; that’s arcing, and you should power the PC off and unplug it immediately. Steady high-pitched whine that has always been there and changes pitch with frame rate is the harmless kind.
How do I know if the coil whine is from my GPU or my power supply?
Use load type as your clue. GPU coil whine tracks frame rate and GPU load — it spikes in game menus and loading screens where the card renders thousands of unnecessary frames, and it changes pitch when you alt-tab or scroll. The cleanest test: set your monitor to 60 Hz and turn on V-Sync (or cap your FPS). If the whine drops or disappears, it’s frame-rate-related and almost certainly the GPU. PSU coil whine instead tracks total system power draw and often appears during heavy CPU-plus-GPU load or even at specific power states, not strictly with FPS. Motherboard VRM whine is rarer, comes from around the CPU socket, and follows CPU load. To pin it physically, roll up a sheet of paper into a tube, hold one end to your ear and point the other at each component in turn — but remember the case itself resonates, so the sound can seem to come from the wrong place.
Does coil whine go away over time?
Sometimes, but it’s not guaranteed. On newer components the whine can genuinely fade over the first few weeks of use as heat cycling and vibration let the coil’s potting material settle and dampen slightly. Plenty of people report a card that whined out of the box being noticeably quieter a month later. That said, just as many cards whine for their entire life. So ‘give it a few weeks’ is a reasonable thing to try before you take action on a new part, but it is not a fix you can count on — don’t buy a card assuming the whine will disappear.
How do I actually reduce coil whine?
The methods that genuinely work all reduce or smooth the electrical load on the coils. First, cap your frame rate — enable V-Sync, turn on G-Sync/FreeSync, or set an FPS limit in your GPU driver so the card stops rendering 900 fps in a menu; this is the single most effective fix because uncapped frame rates are the most common trigger. Second, undervolt the GPU in MSI Afterburner: lowering voltage while holding clocks reduces the current through the inductors and often cuts the whine (it lowers temps too). Third, a high-quality power supply with clean voltage regulation can help if a cheap or aging PSU is the source. What does NOT work: BIOS clock tweaks alone, and — critically — never apply superglue, hot glue, or nail polish to the coils. That common bad tip traps heat, wicks under components, and kills any warranty or repair path — and a conductive material would risk a short outright.
Can I RMA or return a component just for coil whine?
Usually not through a warranty RMA. Because coil whine is considered a normal characteristic of inductors under power, most manufacturers explicitly will not accept an RMA for coil whine alone — several laptop and GPU makers have said as much publicly. Your better lever is the retailer’s return or exchange window. Coil whine varies from unit to unit even within the same model, so if you’re inside a no-questions-asked return period (Amazon, Best Buy, Micro Center), swapping for another copy of the same card is a legitimate roll of the dice — the replacement may be silent. Outside that window, a warranty claim will typically only succeed if the whine comes with an actual fault like crashing or artifacting.
What does coil whine sound like, and how is it different from fan noise?
Coil whine is a high-pitched electronic buzzing, squealing, chirping, or zinging — sharp and ’electrical’ rather than mechanical. Its defining trait is that it changes with electrical load: the pitch shifts when frame rate changes, when you open a menu, or when you alt-tab. Fan noise is different: a whooshing or humming that rises smoothly with fan RPM, or a grinding/rattling/clicking that signals a worn bearing. If the noise tracks your frame counter, it’s coil whine. If it tracks temperature and fan speed, or grinds mechanically, it’s a fan. A gurgling or bubbling is an AIO pump, not a coil. Sorting the sound type first tells you whether you’re chasing a harmless electrical quirk or a mechanical part that’s actually wearing out.
