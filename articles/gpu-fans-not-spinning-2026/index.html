You glance at your open case, the GPU fans are dead still, and the internet immediately splits into two camps: your card is failing, RMA it — or it’s fine, that’s normal, ignore it. Both are sometimes right, and that’s exactly the problem. “GPU fans not spinning” describes three completely different situations, and the fix — or the relief — depends entirely on which one you actually have.

The good news is that for most people asking this question, nothing is wrong. Modern graphics cards stop their fans on purpose. The trick is knowing how to tell that normal, healthy silence apart from a genuinely dead fan, and to spot the one case where fans-not-spinning isn’t a fan issue at all.

Step 1: Idle or under load? This one check sorts everything

Before you touch a screwdriver, answer a single question: are the fans off because the card is cool, or off despite the card being hot?

Since about 2016, nearly every mid-range and higher graphics card ships with a zero-RPM idle feature. You’ll see it under a different brand name depending on who made your card:

MakerFeature name
MSIZero Frozr
ASUS0dB Fan
GigabyteFan Stop
NVIDIA / AMD (reference)Idle Fan Stop / Zero RPM mode

They all do the same thing: below roughly 50-60 °C, the aluminum heatsink alone sheds enough heat that the fans aren’t needed, so the card shuts them off to run completely silent. A GPU sitting on the Windows desktop, browsing, or playing video is usually well under that threshold — so fans-off at idle is the card working exactly as designed, not a failure.

To confirm it in 60 seconds, install HWiNFO (free) and watch the GPU temperature, then load something demanding — a game, or a quick FurMark or OCCT run:

  • Fans start once the temp crosses ~55-60 °C → everything is fine. You were looking at zero-RPM idle. You’re done.
  • Temp climbs past ~65-70 °C and the fans still haven’t moved → the auto curve isn’t triggering them. Now it’s a real fault — continue to Step 2.
  • No picture on screen at all, fans dead from the moment you power on → this usually isn’t a fan problem. Jump to Step 4.

That fork is the whole game. Most searches for this end right here at the first bullet.

Step 2: Fans stay off under load — the real fixes, in order

If the card is genuinely hot and the fans refuse to spin, work these from easiest to hardest. Roughly this is also their hit-rate order.

1. Override the fan curve in software. Open MSI Afterburner (it works on any brand), enable manual fan control, and set a fixed 60-70% speed. If the fans jump to life on command, the hardware is fine and the problem was a stuck auto curve, a disabled setting, or a software conflict — set a sensible custom curve with a low floor and you’re done. If they don’t respond to a direct manual command, the issue is physical; keep going.

2. Re-seat the fan power cable. On most cards the fan assembly connects to the PCB through a small internal header, and it can work loose — especially after shipping, a move, or a recent reinstall. Power down, pull the card, and check that little fan connector is fully clicked in. While the card is out, blow the dust out of the heatsink and fans with a can of compressed air; a packed heatsink can also trip thermal weirdness.

3. Re-seat the card and its PCIe power. Press the card firmly back into the x16 slot until the retention clip clicks, and unplug then firmly re-seat every PCIe power cable at both ends. A card that isn’t getting clean, sufficient power can behave erratically, fans included.

4. Clean-reinstall the driver. A corrupted driver or a bad VBIOS/software profile can leave the fan controller in a bad state. Download the latest driver from NVIDIA or AMD, run Display Driver Uninstaller (DDU) in Safe Mode to wipe the old one, reboot, and install clean. This is also the fix when the fans quit right after a driver update reset your curve.

If the fans still won’t move under load after all four, the fan or its motor is the problem — Step 3.

Step 3: Dead or dying fan — how to be sure, and what to do

A fan that’s mechanically failed gives itself away. With the PC fully powered off, gently flick each fan blade with a finger:

  • Spins freely and coasts → the fan is healthy; your problem was one of the Step-2 causes.
  • Stiff, grinding, or won’t turn → the bearing is worn or seized. This is the textbook cause when one of two or three fans is dead while the others spin — zero-RPM stops all of them together, so a single stationary fan is a hardware fault, not a setting.

A grinding, rattling, or clicking noise in the weeks before a fan stops is the classic worn-bearing warning. (Don’t confuse that mechanical grind with a high-pitched electronic zing that changes with your frame rate — that’s coil whine, a harmless electrical noise, not a dying fan.) For an older card, a worn sleeve bearing that’s lost its lubricant can sometimes be revived with one or two drops of light machine oil (sewing-machine oil is ideal) under the center sticker — never WD-40, which is a solvent that will make it worse over time. That’s a stopgap, not a repair.

The durable fix is a fresh fan. Replacement GPU fan kits for common cards are usually $10-25 and are a straightforward swap if you’re comfortable removing the shroud and matching the connector type and blade count. If the card is still under warranty, don’t open it — RMA it instead; opening the shroud can void coverage.

Step 4: Fans off and no display — this isn’t a fan problem

If the fans are dead from the second you hit the power button and you get no picture at all, stop troubleshooting the fans. Spinning fans only ever prove a card is getting some standby power; the reverse — dead fans with no display — almost always means the GPU isn’t initializing (POSTing) in the first place. The usual culprits are:

  • A PCIe power connector not fully seated — extremely common with the 12VHPWR / 16-pin plug on RTX 40- and 50-series cards, which needs to click all the way home.
  • The card not fully pressed into the slot.
  • The monitor plugged into the motherboard instead of the graphics card’s own ports.
  • A power supply that’s too weak or failing under load.

Re-seat the card until the clip clicks, re-seat every power cable firmly at both ends, and confirm the monitor cable is on the card, not the motherboard. Because this is a different branch entirely, it has its own full walk-through: Graphics Card Not Detected? Find Where It Disappears First.

When to worry — and when not to

Put simply: fans-off is only an emergency when the card is hot. A GPU that idles silent and spins up under load is one of the best-behaved things in your PC. A GPU that bakes past 80 °C while its fans sit dead will throttle, then crash, then eventually protect itself with a thermal shutdown — and running it that way repeatedly shortens its life. If you’ve confirmed a genuinely dead fan, don’t game on the card until it’s fixed; low-load desktop use is fine while you wait on a replacement. (The CPU cooler is the opposite case — it has no safe zero-RPM idle, so a still CPU fan is far more urgent: CPU fan not spinning covers that one.) And the mirror-image failure — fans that refuse to leave full speed — has its own fix order: GPU fans stuck at 100%.

And if the fans turn out to be healthy but the card is still misbehaving — glitches, crashes, black screens — the fan was a red herring and the real fault is elsewhere. Overheating that survives working fans, or visual corruption, points at a different problem: see GPU Artifacts? Sort Software vs Dying VRAM and Fix GPU Driver Crashes.

The quick version

  1. Check idle vs load first. Watch the temp in HWiNFO and load a game or stress test. Fans start by ~55-60 °C = normal zero-RPM mode, nothing’s wrong.
  2. Fans off while hot (65 °C+)? That’s a real fault. Force them in MSI Afterburner; if they respond, it was the curve/software. If not, re-seat the fan cable, re-seat the card + PCIe power, and clean-reinstall the driver with DDU.
  3. Spin each fan by hand (PC off). Stiff or grinding = worn bearing; a single dead fan among spinning ones is hardware, not a setting. Light oil is a stopgap; a $10-25 replacement fan is the fix (RMA instead if in warranty).
  4. Fans dead and no display? Not a fan issue — the card isn’t POSTing. Re-seat card and power, check the 12VHPWR plug, move the monitor to the card’s port.

Sort which of those you actually have before you spend a cent, and you’ll stop RMAing cards that were working perfectly — and stop ignoring a genuinely cooked one because “that’s just zero-RPM mode.”

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal for my GPU fans to not spin?
Yes, usually — if the card is idle. Modern graphics cards (NVIDIA RTX 20-series and newer, AMD RX 5000 and newer) ship with a zero-RPM feature — MSI calls it Zero Frozr, ASUS calls it 0dB Fan, Gigabyte calls it Fan Stop, and NVIDIA and AMD refer to idle fan stop / Zero RPM mode. Below roughly 50-60 °C the heatsink alone handles the heat, so the fans deliberately stay off to run silent. Watching a still desktop with the fans motionless is the single most common ‘my fans are broken’ panic, and it is completely normal. The fans should kick in within a few seconds once you load a game or a stress test and the temperature crosses the threshold. It is only a problem if the fans stay off while the card is hot (roughly 70 °C and up) or under load.
How do I force my GPU fans to spin to test them?
Override the fan curve or raise the temperature. Install MSI Afterburner (it works on any brand of card), enable manual fan control, and either drag the curve up or set a fixed 50-70% speed and apply — healthy fans spin immediately. If they ignore a direct manual command, that rules out zero-RPM mode and points at a connection or hardware fault. Alternatively, just run a GPU stress test like FurMark or OCCT for a minute; as the card heats past about 55-60 °C the fans should engage on their own. Don’t leave a permanent 100% curve for daily use — it’s only a diagnostic. Return to the default (auto) curve once you’ve confirmed the fans respond.
How do I know if my GPU fan is actually broken?
Three tells. First, temperature: watch the GPU temp in HWiNFO — if it climbs past ~60 °C under load and the fans still haven’t started, the auto curve isn’t triggering them and something is wrong. Second, the spin-by-hand test: with the PC fully powered off, gently flick each fan with a finger — a healthy fan spins freely for a moment, a failing one is stiff, grinds, or won’t turn. Third, count them: if one fan spins and another doesn’t, that dead fan has a bad bearing or a broken header, not a zero-RPM setting (zero-RPM stops all of them together). A grinding or rattling noise in the weeks before a fan quits is the classic worn-bearing signature.
Can I run my GPU with the fans not spinning?
At idle, yes — that is zero-RPM mode working as designed and the card stays cool passively. Under load, no — a GPU pushed hard with dead fans heat-soaks within minutes, throttles hard, and eventually hits its thermal-shutdown limit to protect itself, and repeated overheating shortens the card’s life. If one of two or three fans has died you can limp along at low load or with a moderate manual curve on the survivors while you source a replacement, but don’t game on it. If every fan is dead under load, stop using the card until you’ve fixed the cause.
My GPU fans aren't spinning and I get no display — what's wrong?
That is usually not a fan problem at all. When the fans stay off AND you get no picture, the card typically isn’t initializing (POSTing) — the likelier causes are a loose PCIe power connector (very common with the 12VHPWR / 16-pin plug on RTX 40- and 50-series cards), the card not fully seated in the slot, a monitor plugged into the motherboard instead of the card, or a power supply that can’t feed it. Fix that branch first: re-seat the card until the retention clip clicks, re-seat every power cable firmly at both ends, and move the monitor cable to the card’s own port. Full walk-through: Graphics Card Not Detected.
Why did my GPU fans stop spinning after a driver update?
A driver or its bundled software can reset your fan curve or — on AMD cards especially — re-enable a Zero RPM mode you had previously turned off, so fans that used to idle-spin now stop entirely at low load. It is a setting change, not a hardware failure. Re-open your tuning software (AMD Adrenalin’s tuning tab, or MSI Afterburner) and either turn Zero RPM off or set a custom curve with a low floor so the fans always keep a slow spin. If a bad or half-installed driver is the cause, a clean reinstall with DDU (Display Driver Uninstaller) in Safe Mode followed by the latest driver usually restores normal fan behavior.
