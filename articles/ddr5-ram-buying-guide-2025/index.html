Why is DDR5 so expensive right now?

If DDR5 feels like it costs several times what it did a year ago, that is exactly what happened. Memory prices began climbing in the second half of 2025, and the cause is the AI data-center buildout. Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron - the three companies that make essentially all of the world’s DRAM - have shifted wafer capacity toward high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and high-density server DDR5, because that is where the margins are while hyperscalers buy everything they can produce.

Consumer DDR5 competes for the same fabrication lines. When a production line can make a high-margin HBM stack for an AI accelerator or a desktop kit, the desktop kit loses priority. The squeeze falls hardest on the exact 16 GB and 32 GB modules in this guide. A 32GB DDR5-6000 kit that sold for around $80 to $100 in mid-2025 reached roughly $432 by early 2026 - an increase of more than 400% in under a year - and has drifted only slightly since.

What this means for buyers:

  • Don’t wait for a crash. AMD has said publicly it expects DDR5 prices to stay high until around 2028 as new fabs ramp. There is no obvious catalyst for a sharp consumer-DDR5 drop in the near term. Buy when you need it, not on a timing bet.
  • Capacity costs more than speed - the opposite of the old advice. The per-GB surcharge is roughly flat across tiers, so a 64GB kit really does cost about twice a 32GB kit. If 32GB covers your workload, you dodge the steepest part of the curve.
  • Used DDR5 is finally worth a look in a way it wasn’t in 2022 - the standard is mature, and a two-year-old DDR5-6000 kit performs identically to a new one, often at a meaningful discount to shortage-inflated retail.

When did DDR5 come out?

DDR5 launched in late 2021 (November), alongside Intel’s 12th-generation Alder Lake CPUs. It was expensive and scarce at launch - a kit cost roughly triple its DDR4 equivalent - and matured over 2022 to 2024 into the cheap, default standard. Ironically, 2026’s AI-driven shortage has pushed prices back toward that early-adopter premium, this time for supply reasons rather than newness. By 2026, DDR5 is the only option on every current CPU platform (AMD AM5, Intel LGA 1851).

DDR4 vs DDR5 in 2026: do you even have a choice?

For anyone building a new PC in 2026, this mostly answers itself: current platforms are DDR5-only. AMD’s AM5 socket (Ryzen 7000 and 9000) and Intel’s LGA 1851 (Core Ultra) accept DDR5 exclusively. DDR4 now lives on only on older AM4 and LGA 1700 boards - the territory of budget builds and second-hand bargains. If you are sitting on one of those boards and wondering whether to jump, memory is now the deciding cost: the 5800X3D to 9800X3D upgrade math prices the whole AM4-to-AM5 move at today’s DDR5 rates.

Are they interchangeable? Not even slightly. DDR5 puts the keying notch in a different position than DDR4, so a DDR5 stick physically will not seat in a DDR4 slot, and vice versa. The voltage, the on-module power management, and the dual-subchannel architecture are all different. There is no adapter, and no current board takes both types in the same slots.

Should you upgrade a working DDR4 system to DDR5? Only as part of a full platform move - new CPU and motherboard, not RAM alone. On a like-for-like CPU, going from good DDR4-3600 to DDR5-6000 is a single-digit gaming uplift. In a shortage that makes a 32GB DDR5 kit cost $400+, that is an especially bad trade. If you’re content on a 5800X3D or 12700K with solid DDR4, there is no RAM-only upgrade worth the money right now - put it toward a GPU instead.

The speed vs performance reality

Every RAM manufacturer wants you to believe higher MT/s numbers translate directly to better performance. The benchmark data tells a much less exciting story - which matters more than ever now that the price gap between speed tiers is small and the capacity gap is huge.

Gaming: 0-4% between 6000 and 7200

PC Gamer tested DDR5-7200 CL34 against DDR5-6000 CL30 across multiple titles. The result in most games: 0-4% difference in average FPS - within run-to-run variance in some cases. The one outlier was Baldur’s Gate 3, historically memory-sensitive, at roughly +4% averages and +8% in 1% lows at 7200. That single game doesn’t justify a premium on RAM for a build that plays twenty other titles.

The takeaway for gaming: anything between DDR5-6000 CL30 and DDR5-6400 CL32 gets you effectively the same frame rates. Faster kits exist. They cost a bit more. You won’t feel the difference.

Productivity: 4-7% at most, one 12% outlier

Puget Systems tested the fastest DDR5 kits against baseline JEDEC speeds (DDR5-4800) in professional workloads. The improvement topped out at 4% on Intel and 7% on AMD versus stock JEDEC timings. One workload - Unreal Engine shader compilation - showed a 12% improvement at the highest tested speeds. Everything else clustered in the 2-5% range.

A critical finding from that same testing: AMD platforms couldn’t complete benchmark runs at DDR5-6400 in their configuration. The AMD memory controller is more sensitive to frequency than Intel’s, which is why AMD’s own recommendation lands at 6000 - not because 6000 is magic, but because going higher introduces instability risks that aren’t worth the 2-3% you might gain if it works.

The 6000-6400 sweet spot - what to actually buy

The optimal DDR5 purchase in 2026 sits between DDR5-6000 CL30 and DDR5-6400 CL32. This range maximizes the performance-per-dollar curve, works reliably on both Intel and AMD platforms, and avoids the instability risks of pushing higher frequencies. The specific kit recommendations

32GB: buy the cheapest reliable DDR5-6000, not the fastest

In a normal market you might chase a tighter-binned kit. In the 2026 shortage, the smart move is the opposite: get any reputable 32GB DDR5-6000 kit at the lowest in-stock price and put the difference toward your GPU or storage. The Lexar THOR at ~$449 is currently the cheapest proven name-brand 6000 kit; the Patriot Viper Venom CL30 gets you the same tighter primary timings when it is back in stock (it is currently sold out). The G.Skill Flare X5 and Corsair Vengeance at ~$470 are the safe, proven picks on AMD QVLs and carry thousands of reviews - pay the small premium only if the cheaper kits are out of stock or off-QVL for your board.

What you should not do is pay up for a DDR5-7200 showcase kit. In today’s market that money buys 0-1% more gaming FPS and a nicer heatspreader. It is far better spent elsewhere.

64GB: only if a real workload needs it

64GB (2 x 32GB) DDR5-6000 kits start near $875 and stock is genuinely thin - many DDR5-6000 desktop SKUs show as out of stock, and you may end up on a slower 5600 kit to get 64GB in stock. For a workstation running VMs, compiling code, or editing video, 64GB is worth it. For a pure gaming build, it is not - 32GB is the baseline, and the shortage makes over-buying capacity the single most expensive mistake on the parts list. If you’re on Ryzen 7000/9000, lean toward a 6000-speed 64GB kit for stability; 4-DIMM configurations are worse (see the gotcha below).

96GB and up: platform-dependent, pay the premium only with a reason

96GB (2 x 48GB) DDR5-6000 runs about $1,310 now. The platform difference matters more than the kit difference at this capacity - Intel’s memory controller handles 2 x 48GB DIMMs more gracefully than AMD’s at speed. If you’re doing large-scale content creation, local AI, or heavy virtualization, it exists and it works. If you’re not sure you need it, you don’t - and at shortage pricing, 96GB you don’t use is the most expensive empty capacity you can buy.

The gotchas nobody warns you about

XMP and EXPO are overclocks

XMP 3.0 (Intel) and EXPO (AMD) profiles are not guaranteed to work. They are overclock profiles stored in the DIMM’s SPD. Your motherboard, your specific CPU’s memory controller, and your luck in the silicon lottery all determine whether a given profile runs stable. If a DDR5-6400 kit doesn’t post at rated speed on your board, that is not a defective kit - it is the reality of overclocking. Always check your motherboard’s QVL (Qualified Vendor List) before buying, especially for 2 x 24GB or 2 x 48GB kits, which are more finicky than 16GB and 32GB modules.

Four DIMMs kill your rated speed

If your motherboard has four DIMM slots and you populate all four, expect your maximum stable speed to drop significantly - often 400-800 MT/s below what the same kit runs on two slots. Memory controllers are stressed harder with four DIMMs. If you need 64GB, buy a 2 x 32GB kit, not 4 x 16GB. Same capacity, better performance, and you leave two slots open.

The price spread is wild - and worse during a shortage

The same kit can show a $200-plus spread across sellers, and shortages make third-party marketplace gouging far worse. Always verify you’re buying from a first-party authorized seller. Check PCPartPicker or the manufacturer’s product page for legitimate retail links. A kit that costs $450 from an authorized seller can list at $650-plus from a marketplace vendor - and during a shortage, panicked buyers pay it. Don’t.

The verdict

DDR5-6000 to 6400 is still the smart-money speed in 2026 - but the shortage rewrote the buying rule. The benchmark data is clear: the jump from JEDEC 4800 to 6000 is meaningful, and the jump from 6000 to 7200 is a rounding error. What changed is the price structure. Speed is now nearly free; capacity is the expense. So buy the cheapest reliable 32GB DDR5-6000 kit (the Lexar THOR at ~$450 today, or the Patriot Viper Venom CL30 when it is back in stock), take exactly the capacity your workload needs and no more, and put every saved dollar toward the components that actually move the needle - your GPU and your storage.

Check your motherboard’s QVL. Buy from authorized sellers. Use two DIMMs, not four. Enable XMP or EXPO in BIOS - the rated speed is not active until you do. And don’t wait for a crash that the people who make the chips say isn’t coming until 2028.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a 32GB DDR5 kit cost right now (August 2026)?
As of an August 1, 2026 check, the cheapest in-stock 32GB (2 x 16GB) DDR5-6000 kit sits near $375 to $400, and CL30-timed 32GB kits typically run $400 to $480. That is roughly 4 to 5x the ~$80 to $100 those kits cost in mid-2025, driven by the 2026 AI-fueled DRAM shortage. Prices eased slightly off the early-2026 peak but remain historically high, so check the live retailer links before buying rather than trusting any fixed number.
How much does a 64GB DDR5 kit cost in 2026?
The August 1, 2026 re-check priced the 32GB tier directly; 64GB (2 x 32GB) DDR5-6000 tracks the same per-GB rate, which lands it in the $800s, and stock is thin with many desktop SKUs showing out of stock. Expect to pay roughly twice the going 32GB rate, because 64GB uses the same high-density modules the AI-driven shortage has hit hardest. If 32GB covers your workload, buying it now dodges the steepest part of the price curve.
What is the current DDR5 price per GB in 2026?
In early August 2026, mainstream DDR5-6000 works out to roughly $12 to $14 per gigabyte, priced at the 32GB tier: about $12.5/GB on a $400 kit, or near $11.7/GB at the $375 in-stock floor. The 64GB and 96GB tiers were not re-priced individually in this check and are quoted as bands at the same rate. For reference, the lowest-ever price on a 32GB DDR5-6000 kit was about $2.25 per gigabyte ($72). So today’s shopper is paying five to six times the historical floor per gig.
Is DDR5-6000 still the sweet spot in 2026?
Yes for AMD Ryzen 7000/9000 - DDR5-6000 keeps the memory controller in AMD’s recommended 1:1 mode (UCLK matching the memory clock; on AM5 the Infinity Fabric runs its own independent clock). For Intel 13th-15th gen, DDR5-6400 or 6800 is the modern sweet spot since Intel’s memory controller is less ratio-sensitive. Above DDR5-7200 the latency penalty and cost premium consistently outweigh the bandwidth gain in real games.
How much DDR5 RAM do I need in 2026?
32 GB is the new baseline for gaming and content creation. 16 GB is the floor for casual use only. 64 GB makes sense for VMs, large Lightroom catalogs, or AI workloads on a GPU with limited VRAM. During the shortage, capacity is the single most expensive variable, so buy the amount you actually need rather than over-provisioning on speculation.
CL30 vs CL36 - does the timing matter?
Slightly. At DDR5-6000, CL30 is ~2 ns faster than CL36 in absolute latency (10 ns vs 12 ns first-word), which shows up as 1-3% in benchmarks like 7-Zip, code compile, and 1% lows in CPU-bound games. If a CL30 kit costs $10-20 more than CL36 at the same speed, take CL30. If it’s $40+ more, the gap is not worth it - and during the shortage that spread is often wider than the performance justifies.
Is XMP / EXPO required to hit advertised speeds?
Yes. DDR5 modules JEDEC-default to DDR5-4800 or 5600 - the rated speed (6000, 6400, etc.) only applies after enabling XMP (Intel) or EXPO (AMD) in BIOS. This is the single most common reason a $400 RAM kit feels slow: the buyer forgot the BIOS toggle.
Should I wait for DDR5 prices to drop before buying?
Probably not, if you need the RAM now. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron have shifted capacity toward high-bandwidth memory for AI accelerators, and AMD has publicly said it expects DDR5 prices to stay elevated until roughly 2028 as new fabs come online. Analysts see no sharp consumer-DDR5 crash before late 2026 at the earliest. Buy when you need it, not on a timing bet - and buy only the capacity you’ll use.
Can you mix DDR4 and DDR5 RAM?
No. DDR4 and DDR5 use a different keying notch position, different voltage, and a different module architecture, so a DDR5 stick physically will not seat in a DDR4 slot and there is no adapter that bridges them. A motherboard supports one type or the other, never both in the same slots. The only boards that appear to take ‘both’ are a handful of early LGA1700 models sold in separate DDR4-only and DDR5-only versions; you still pick one type per board.
When did DDR5 come out, and what actually changed from DDR4?
DDR5 is the fifth generation of DDR memory, launched in late 2021 (November) alongside Intel’s 12th-gen Alder Lake. Versus DDR4 it roughly doubles base bandwidth (4800+ MT/s vs 3200), splits each DIMM into two independent 32-bit subchannels, moves power management onto the module via an on-board PMIC, and adds basic on-die ECC. By 2026 it is the mature default on every current CPU platform.
