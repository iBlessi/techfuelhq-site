You press the power button and nothing happens — no fans, no lights, no sound — and the obvious conclusion is that the button is broken. It usually isn’t. On a desktop, “power button not working” is one of the most mis-diagnosed faults there is, because the same dead-press symptom is produced by a loose wire, a flipped PSU switch, stuck residual power, or a failing power supply just as often as by an actually-broken button. Swap the button (or the whole case) on that assumption and half the time the machine is still dead.

So the real first move isn’t “how do I replace the power button” — it’s “is it even the button?” There’s a single test that answers that in about a minute, before you spend a dollar or order a part. Let’s get to it in the right order.

First: totally dead, or partly alive?

Look at the machine when you press power and sort it into one of three buckets — this decides everything that follows:

  • Nothing at all. No fans twitch, no LEDs, no motherboard debug light, no sound. This is a power-on-path problem — the button, its wire, the PSU switch/cable, flea power, or a dead PSU. That’s what this guide is for.
  • Fans spin or lights come on, but nothing else. The PC is powering on — the button is working. Your problem is downstream (no POST, no display), which is a different track: start with PC Turns On But No Display? Find Where the Boot Breaks.
  • The button physically sticks, feels mushy, or stays pressed in. That’s a mechanical button fault — jump to the “clean or replace the button” section below.

The rest of this walkthrough is about the first bucket: you press power and the PC is stone dead.

Step 1: the 60-second differential

Before touching anything, use the pattern of the symptom to rank the suspects. These are the tells that separate a button problem from everything it gets blamed for:

Notice how few of these rows are actually “the button.” That’s the whole point — and the next three steps walk the ladder from free-and-likely to conclusive.

Step 2: rule out power delivery (free, do this first)

Most “dead button” calls are solved here without opening anything meaningful:

  • Check the PSU’s rear switch. The rocker on the back of the power supply has a | (on) and O (off). It’s easy to knock to O while moving cables or the tower. Set it to |.
  • Test the wall outlet and cable. Plug a lamp or phone charger into the same outlet to confirm it’s live, and reseat the power cable firmly at both the wall and the PSU. Try a different cable if you have one.
  • Drain the flea power. Residual charge can leave the board in a stuck, unresponsive state. Turn off the PSU switch, unplug the power cable from the wall, then hold the case power button down for 30 seconds to bleed the remaining charge out of the capacitors. Plug back in, flip the PSU switch to |, and try again. This one step revives a surprising number of “dead” PCs. Intel lists the same flea-power drain as a first move when a computer won’t turn on.

If the machine springs back to life after any of these, the button was never broken — you had a power-delivery hiccup. If it’s still dead, it’s time for the test that actually settles the button question.

Step 3: the conclusive test — jump the header pins

This is the single most useful step, because it tells you definitively whether the button is at fault. The case’s power button does exactly one thing: it briefly connects two pins on the motherboard’s front-panel header. You can do that connection yourself.

Read this before you do it: this is the one step in the guide performed on a live board, so touch only the two PWR_SW pins, briefly, and be careful not to slip onto neighboring pins — a screwdriver dragged across the front-panel header or the board can short something you did not intend. Keep your hands clear of the CPU and GPU power connectors. If you are not confident identifying the pins, check the manual’s front-panel diagram first rather than guessing.

  1. Leave the PC plugged in and the PSU switch on (|).
  2. Remove the side panel.
  3. Find the front-panel header — a small cluster of pins, usually in the lower-right corner of the board. Two of them are labeled PWR_SW (or PWR / PW / Power SW). Your motherboard manual shows the exact layout; the two power-switch pins are what you want.
  4. Briefly touch those two PWR_SW pins together with the tip of a flathead screwdriver — a quick tap, metal across both pins at once.

What the result tells you:

  • The PC powers up. The motherboard, PSU, and all power delivery are working. The fault is the physical button or the thin wire running from it to the header — the cheapest, easiest failure on the whole list. Go to Step 4.
  • Still nothing — no fans, no lights, no spin-up. The button is now ruled out. The problem is behind it: a PSU that’s failed, a dislodged 24-pin or 8-pin CPU power cable, or a motherboard fault. Skip to “when it isn’t the button.”

Intel documents this screwdriver method explicitly — lightly touching the two power-switch header pins turns the system on if everything behind them is connected correctly, doing the same job as the case button. It’s a standard bench move, not a hack.

Step 4: the free permanent fix — steal the reset switch

If the jump test booted the PC, you’ve confirmed the button/wire is dead — but you don’t necessarily need to buy anything. The reset switch is electrically identical to the power switch: both are momentary buttons that briefly short two header pins. So you can promote the reset button to power duty:

  1. Power down and unplug.
  2. At the front-panel header, disconnect the small two-pin RESET_SW cable and the PWR_SW cable.
  3. Plug the Reset cable onto the pins the Power cable was using (the PWR_SW pins).
  4. Reassemble. Now the case’s reset button turns the PC on.

You give up the reset function — rarely missed — and gain a working power control for free. Builders on r/buildapc and Tom’s Hardware point to this exact swap as the go-to when a case power button dies. If you’d rather have a proper button back, a replacement PC power/reset switch is a couple of dollars and plugs straight onto the same pins.

If the button feels stuck or gritty

When the button physically sticks — stays depressed, feels mushy, or needs a hard jab — the switch mechanism is fouled or worn, not electrically dead. With the PC off and unplugged, blast compressed air around the gap of the button to clear dust or debris, and work it a few times. If it still sticks, the spring or switch is done — swap to the reset button as above, or replace the switch.

When it isn’t the button

If shorting the PWR_SW pins produced nothing at all, the button is cleared and the fault is deeper in the power chain. Work these in order:

  • Reseat the main power cables. With the PC unplugged, firmly re-seat the 24-pin motherboard connector and the 8-pin (4+4) CPU power connector at the top of the board. A partially-seated CPU power cable is a classic “totally dead, no response” cause after a build or a component swap.
  • Suspect the power supply. A PSU that’s died — or whose rear switch/cable is bad — gives you exactly this no-response-at-all symptom. The clean way to confirm it is covered in PSU Failure Symptoms: Is It Really the Power Supply?, including why the paperclip test only proves a PSU can switch on, not that it’s healthy.
  • Then the motherboard. If power delivery checks out and jumping the pins still does nothing, board-level failure is what’s left. The signs and the how-do-I-be-sure steps live in Motherboard Failure Symptoms: How to Be Sure.

And if at any point the PC does start powering on — fans and lights — but won’t show anything or finish booting, you’ve moved past the power-button problem into a display/POST issue: PC Turns On But No Display? picks up there. If it powers on but then restarts or cuts out on its own, work PC Keeps Restarting? Through the Causes instead.

The quick version

  1. Sort the symptom. Totally dead (this guide) vs powers-on-but-no-display (a different problem) vs button physically stuck (mechanical).
  2. Rule out power delivery first, free: PSU rear switch to |, test the outlet/cable, and drain flea power (unplug, hold the button 30 seconds, plug back in).
  3. Jump the header pins. Tap the two PWR_SW pins with a screwdriver. Boots = the button/wire is the fault. Nothing = the button is ruled out.
  4. If it’s the button, fix it free: swap the reset switch wire onto the PWR_SW pins, or drop in a couple-dollar replacement button. Sticky button? Compressed air first.
  5. If jumping the pins does nothing, it’s power delivery — reseat the 24-pin and CPU power cables, then check the PSU, then the motherboard.

The button is the cheapest thing that can cause a dead-press, and one of the least likely — so prove it with the screwdriver test before you replace anything. Five minutes of diagnosis here saves you from ordering a case, a PSU, or a board to fix a problem that turned out to be a wire that fell off a pin.

Frequently asked questions

How do I turn my PC on if the power button doesn't work?
You can start the machine directly from the motherboard, bypassing the button entirely. Power down at the wall, open the side panel, and find the front-panel header — a small block of pins in the lower corner of the board, with two of them labeled PWR_SW or PWR (the power switch). With the PC plugged in and the PSU switched on, briefly touch those two pins together with the tip of a flathead screwdriver. If the system powers up, the board and PSU are fine and the fault is in the button or its wire. This is the same momentary connection the case button makes; Intel documents the exact screwdriver method for turning a system on this way. As a permanent free workaround, you can move the case’s Reset switch wire onto the PWR_SW pins and use the reset button to turn the PC on.
How do I know if it's the power button or the motherboard?
Do the screwdriver jump test on the two PWR_SW header pins. If shorting those pins boots the PC, the motherboard and power supply are working and the problem is the physical button or the thin wire that runs from it to the header — cheap and easy to fix. If shorting the pins does nothing at all — no fans, no lights, no spin-up — then the button has been ruled out and you’re looking at a power-delivery problem instead: a PSU that isn’t switched on or has died, a dislodged 24-pin or CPU power cable, or a motherboard fault. In other words, the jump test is the single step that cleanly separates a button problem from a board or PSU problem.
Why did my PC power button suddenly stop working?
The most common causes, roughly in order: the small front-panel wire came loose from the PWR_SW header pins (very common after moving the case, cleaning it out, or a recent build), the PSU’s rear switch got knocked to the off position or the wall outlet is dead, residual ‘flea power’ has the board in a stuck state, the physical button’s spring or contacts wore out or gummed up with dust, or a component further back — the power supply or motherboard — has actually failed. Note the pattern: a genuinely broken button is only one item on that list, and often not the likeliest. That’s why testing beats replacing the button on a guess.
Can I use the reset button to turn my PC on instead?
Yes, and it’s the standard free fix when the power button itself is dead. The power switch and the reset switch are electrically identical — both are simple momentary buttons that briefly short two header pins. Power down and unplug, disconnect the small two-pin Reset (RESET_SW) cable and the Power (PWR_SW) cable from the front-panel header, then plug the Reset cable onto the pins that the Power cable was using. Now the case’s reset button turns the computer on. You lose the reset function, but you get a working power control without buying anything. Builders on r/buildapc and Tom’s Hardware recommend exactly this swap when a case button fails.
What causes a faulty power button?
The button on a desktop case is a small momentary switch, and a few things kill it: dust, grime, or spilled liquid working into the contact so it sticks or stops registering; the tiny return spring wearing out so the button stays depressed or feels mushy; the thin wire between the button and the motherboard header pulling loose or breaking; or, on an older case, the switch simply reaching the end of its mechanical life. All of these are cheap to deal with — a blast of compressed air, reseating the wire, swapping to the reset switch, or a couple-dollar replacement button — which is exactly why it’s worth confirming the button is the culprit before assuming the worse and pricier options.
How can I tell if my PC is bricked or just won't power on?
‘Bricked’ means the machine powers on but is non-functional — usually a corrupted BIOS/firmware, where fans spin and lights come on but there’s no display or POST. ‘Won’t power on’ is the opposite: nothing happens at all when you hit the button — no fans, no lights, no sound. If the screwdriver jump test and a flea-power drain get the system spinning up again, it was never bricked; it was a power-on-path problem (button, cable, PSU switch). If the PC does power up but never shows anything on screen or completes POST, that’s a different track — a display or boot fault rather than a power-button one. Genuine bricking is rare and specific to firmware; most ‘dead’ PCs are a power-delivery or button issue that testing will surface.
