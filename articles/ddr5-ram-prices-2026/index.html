If you searched for what a 32GB DDR5-6000 CL30 kit costs, the answer on August 1, 2026 is roughly $400 to $420, with the cheapest listing at $399.99. That is the number this page exists to give you, with the date attached and a link to where it came from, because a memory price without a date is close to useless in 2026.

16GB: the cheapest entry, and the worst per-gigabyte deal

Newegg’s 16GB DDR5-6000 CL30 listings on August 1, 2026 opened with a single Patriot Viper Venom 16GB module (PVV516G60C30) at $214.99, timings 30-40-40-76 at 1.35V. The cheapest two-stick 16GB kit in that same listing was G.SKILL’s Flare X5 2x8GB at $264.99.

Divide those out and 16GB is the tier that punishes you. The $214.99 module is about $13.44 per gigabyte by my arithmetic. The 32GB kit below is $12.50 per gigabyte. You are paying more per gigabyte for less memory, and the cheapest 16GB option here is a single module rather than a kit, so a two-slot board runs with one of its memory channels empty until you buy a second stick.

Buy 16GB only if the budget is hard-capped and you know the machine’s job. Anything that touches modern games, browser tabs by the dozen, or a container or two has outgrown it. Our breakdown of how much RAM you actually need walks the capacity question by workload rather than by price.

32GB DDR5-6000 CL30: the number most people came for

This is the tier the search traffic is asking about, so here is the full listing spread from Newegg’s DDR5-6000 CL30 results on August 1, 2026:

The XPG LANCER 2x16GB (AX5U6000C3016G-DCLABK) was the cheapest at $399.99, carrying Newegg’s own “lowest price in 30 days” flag. The RGB version of the same kit and Patriot’s Viper Elite 5 2x16GB both listed at $409.99. V-COLOR’s Manta XSky was $419.99. From there the branded tier climbs fast: Team T-Force Delta RGB at $489.99, Corsair Vengeance RGB at $509.99, and G.SKILL’s Trident Z5 Neo RGB at $599.99. Those are list prices; several carried stacking promo codes at the time of the check, so the number at checkout can land below the number on the tile.

That $200 spread between the cheapest and most expensive 32GB CL30 kit is the single most actionable fact on this page. Every kit in it carries the same rated speed and the same rated latency. In a normal market, paying up for a familiar brand is a defensible $30 decision. At current pricing it is a $200 one.

Cross-checking against a second source: WhereIsMyRam’s US price chart listed the DDR5-6000 CL30 32GB minimum at $400 on the same day, within a dollar of Newegg’s floor. The tracker aggregates US retail listings and states that Amazon prices are excluded from its charts, which is why I use it as the independent check here.

Two more figures from that tracker worth knowing before you buy a speed grade: DDR5-6000 CL36 sat at $380 against $400 for CL30, so tightening the timings at 6000 cost about $20 at this capacity. DDR5-6400 CL32 was $390 and DDR5-5600 CL36 was $376.

64GB: double the capacity for slightly more than double the money

Newegg’s 64GB DDR5-6000 CL30 listings on August 1, 2026 bottomed out at V-COLOR’s Manta XFinity RGB 2x32GB (TMXFL3260830KWK) at $869.99. G.SKILL’s Trident Z5 Neo RGB was $995.55, Kingston’s FURY Beast $1,049.99, and Corsair’s Vengeance 2x32GB $1,159.99.

At $869.99 the 64GB tier runs about $13.59 per gigabyte, roughly 9% worse per gigabyte than the 32GB kit. So the honest framing is not “64GB costs twice as much as 32GB.” It costs about 2.2 times as much, and the extra 32GB you are buying arrives at a worse rate than the first 32GB did.

Ignore that penalty if a real workload needs the capacity: virtual machines, large video timelines, a local model that spills out of VRAM. Do not accept it to buy 64GB “to be safe” on a gaming build. At these prices, unused capacity is the most expensive thing on a parts list.

128GB: the tier where the math breaks

Two very different products both get sold as 128GB, and the gap between them is enormous.

Going through 2x64GB modules, Newegg’s 128GB DDR5-6000 listings started at V-COLOR’s Manta XFinity RGB at $2,999.99, and note that it is CL32 rather than CL30 (1.35V, 32-45-45-96). G.SKILL’s Flare X5 and Ripjaws S5 2x64GB kits were $3,249.99 at CL34, and the Trident Z5 Neo RGB was $3,299.99. That $2,999.99 entry point is about $23.44 per gigabyte, nearly double the 32GB rate.

Going through four 32GB modules instead, the same 128GB kit search showed A-Tech’s 4x32GB DDR5-5600 CL46 kit at $1,858.33 and Kingston’s FURY Beast 4x32GB DDR5-5200 CL40 at $2,399.99. The A-Tech kit is roughly $14.52 per gigabyte, which puts it back in line with the rest of the ladder and saves about $1,142 against the 2x64GB route.

The catch is real, not theoretical. You give up the 6000 speed grade, you accept looser timings, and you fill all four DIMM slots, which our DDR5 buying guide covers as the configuration that most reliably costs you rated speed. For a workstation or homelab box where capacity is the entire point and memory clock is not, that trade is usually correct. For anything where the RAM speed matters, the 2x64GB premium is what the convenience costs.

What moved over the last month

WhereIsMyRam’s US market summary on August 1, 2026 showed a 7-day change of +0.9% and a 30-day change of +13.7%, across 1,293 in-stock references, and labeled the week a strong upward trend. The tracker follows 2x16GB kits, and the cheapest tracked configuration at any speed was the $376 DDR5-5600 CL36 entry above.

That 30-day figure is a market-wide aggregate across those 1,293 references, not the move on any one kit. The tracked market rose nearly 14% while a buyer waited.

The honest complication comes from the same tracker: at the individual configuration level it describes DDR5-6000 CL30 as trading at its cheapest level in recent weeks, and Newegg flags the $399.99 XPG LANCER as its own lowest price in 30 days. Both are true. Specific SKUs dip inside a rising market when retailers clear individual lines. Read that as a reason to buy a well-priced kit today, not as a tier that keeps falling.

Buy now or wait: the call, and the reasoning

Buy when you need it. Not because prices are good, but because the supply picture that sets them has no near-term fix.

TrendForce’s July 30, 2026 memory outlook puts the DRAM sufficiency ratio at about -1% to -2% for 2026 and expects the supply-demand gap to widen further in 2027, with DRAM prices on an upward trajectory. Its structural explanation matters more than the forecast: HBM manufacturing requires significantly more wafer input than conventional DRAM, so higher wafer starts do not translate proportionally into additional bit output. More fab capacity does not automatically mean more DIMMs.

Notably, TrendForce splits the two halves of the memory market in that same release. NAND flash supply loosens in the second half of 2027 as new capacity arrives and consumer demand stays weak. DRAM does not. If you have been assuming RAM and SSDs recover together, that assumption is now explicitly contradicted by the forecaster.

The independent read agrees. PCWorld’s April 24, 2026 investigation into the softening that showed up in some regional markets argued the dips were inventory and demand-elasticity effects on top of a structurally tight market rather than a real reversal. Analyst Patrick Moorhead, quoted in that piece, expected conditions to start improving in Q4 2027 and said he did not think the market would be, in his words, “normalized until 2030.”

So the timing bet is not “wait a month.” Taking the earlier of those two dates, it is “wait until late 2027.” If your build can genuinely sit idle for five quarters, waiting is defensible. If it cannot, you are paying an option premium on a market two named sources expect to keep climbing.

When NOT to upgrade

The buy-now advice above applies to memory you actually need. It is not an argument for touching a working machine. Four cases where the right move is to leave it alone:

You are on 32GB and nothing is swapping. Open your task manager under your real workload, not while idle. If committed memory sits below your installed capacity, more RAM buys you nothing. At $400 a kit, “future-proofing” is the most expensive placebo in a 2026 build.

You are running DDR4 and it is stable. A DDR4-to-DDR5 move is a platform change: new CPU, new board, new memory. At $399.99 for the 32GB kit, the memory line alone is now one of the largest items in that upgrade. Unless the CPU is the actual bottleneck, that money does more work in a GPU.

You want to go from CL36 to CL30 at the same capacity. The timing difference costs about $20 when you are already buying, per the tracker figures above. Buying a whole replacement kit to get it means paying $400 to retire working memory. Take tighter timings on a purchase you were making anyway, never as a standalone upgrade.

You are adding a second module to reach an odd total. Memory is sold in matched kits because the modules in one are validated to run together at their rated profile; two sticks bought separately carry no such validation. At current prices a pairing that will not hold its rated speed is an expensive experiment. If you need more, buy a matched kit and sell the old one.

How these numbers were checked

Every price on this page was read on August 1, 2026 from a public retail listing page or an independent price tracker, and each one links to the exact page it came from. Newegg listings supplied the per-capacity figures; WhereIsMyRam supplied the independent cross-check and the market movement figures. The per-gigabyte figures are my own division of those listed prices by capacity, shown so you can check the arithmetic.

Two limits, stated plainly. The retailer links are search pages, so they show today’s prices rather than the ones printed here, which means these exact figures are not reproducible later from the link. And these are list prices: promo codes moved several kits below their listed number on the day I checked, so treat every figure here as a ceiling.

There is no first-party bench data on this page and no test bench behind it. This is a dated price observation, which is what the question calls for. Prices at these retailers move daily. Treat the figures here as the shape of the market on the date printed, and confirm the live number at the link before you spend.

The bottom line

As of August 1, 2026, DDR5-6000 CL30 runs about $12.50 per gigabyte at 32GB, $13.44 at 16GB and $13.59 at 64GB. At 128GB the cheapest DDR5-6000 option is a CL32 kit at $23.44 per gigabyte, or about $14.52 if you drop to a 4x32GB DDR5-5600 kit. The 32GB tier is both the volume sweet spot and the best rate on the ladder, which is unusual and worth taking advantage of.

Buy the capacity your workload actually uses, take the cheapest listing that meets your spec rather than the familiar brand, and do not wait for a correction that the supply forecasts do not support. Frequently asked questions

How much is a 32GB DDR5-6000 CL30 kit in August 2026?
On an August 1, 2026 check of Newegg’s DDR5-6000 CL30 listings, the cheapest 32GB (2x16GB) kit was the XPG LANCER at $399.99, flagged by Newegg as its lowest price in 30 days. Patriot’s Viper Elite 5 and XPG’s RGB LANCER both listed at $409.99, and V-COLOR’s Manta XSky at $419.99. The US tracker WhereIsMyRam put the DDR5-6000 CL30 32GB minimum at $400 the same day. These are list prices before any retailer promo codes.
Is 16GB of DDR5 actually cheaper than 32GB?
In absolute dollars yes, per gigabyte no. The cheapest 16GB DDR5-6000 CL30 option on Newegg on August 1, 2026 was a single Patriot Viper Venom module at $214.99, which works out to about $13.44 per gigabyte. The $399.99 32GB kit works out to $12.50 per gigabyte. You save roughly $185 up front, pay a premium per gigabyte, and give up dual-channel operation if you run one module.
Why is 128GB DDR5 so much more expensive per gigabyte?
Because 64GB DIMMs are a low-volume part. On August 1, 2026 the cheapest 128GB DDR5-6000 kit on Newegg was V-COLOR’s 2x64GB Manta XFinity RGB at $2,999.99 (CL32), about $23.44 per gigabyte. Reaching 128GB with four 32GB modules costs far less per gigabyte: A-Tech’s 4x32GB DDR5-5600 CL46 kit listed at $1,858.33, roughly $14.52 per gigabyte, in exchange for a slower rated speed and a four-DIMM configuration.
Should I buy DDR5 now or wait for prices to fall?
Buy when you need it. TrendForce’s July 30, 2026 outlook puts the DRAM sufficiency ratio at about -1% to -2% for 2026 and expects the supply-demand gap to widen in 2027, with prices on an upward trajectory. Analyst Patrick Moorhead told PCWorld in April 2026 that conditions should start improving in Q4 2027 but would not normalize until 2030. Waiting a quarter is a bet against both.
