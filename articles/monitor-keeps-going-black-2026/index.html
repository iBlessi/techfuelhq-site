Quick answer

Let the recovery pattern name the cause. A screen that flickers to black then returns is a signal dropout: a marginal cable, a driver crash, or PCIe link power. One that stays black, or only drops under load, points to heat, an unstable overclock, or VRR flicker. Black only after sleep is DisplayPort hot-plug, not a dying monitor.

A monitor that keeps cutting to black - usually for a second or two, then recovering - is one of the most common and most misdiagnosed PC problems. People assume the monitor is dying and start shopping for a new one, when the actual cause is almost always a cable, a driver, or a power setting. The good news is that you already have the single most useful clue and probably haven’t used it: the rhythm of the blackout - how it drops and how it comes back - tells you which of five very different faults you’re looking at. Read the rhythm first, and you fix it at the cheapest step instead of replacing hardware that’s fine.

Read the blackout's rhythm
Think of the video signal as a trace on a scope: up is a live picture, the dotted floor is black. How it drops and whether it climbs back names the fault - before you touch a cable.
Flickers, then recovers
A signal dropout: a cable that can't hold your res/refresh, a driver crash-and-recover (TDR), or PCIe link power. Steps 1-3.
Drops and stays black
A hard fault that needs a reboot or power-cycle to clear: heat, a loose GPU, or power delivery. Step 4.

First, read the symptom

The scope trace above, in words - the exact behavior tells you where to look:

  • Goes black and recovers on its own (a flicker to black, then the picture returns) → a signal or driver dropout. Cable, driver, or power settings. Start at step 1.
  • Goes black and stays black until you reboot or power-cycle → a harder fault. More likely overheating, an unstable overclock, a loose GPU, or power delivery. Jump to step 4.
  • Only goes black in games / under load → heat, power stability, or VRR flicker, not the cable. Go to step 4, and check the VRR section.
  • Only goes black waking from sleep → DisplayPort hot-plug behavior, not a dying monitor. See the after-sleep section.
  • Never displayed at all (black from the moment you power on, and it never showed a picture this session) → that’s not an intermittent-blackout problem, it’s a no-POST one. See PC turns on but no display instead.

Step 1: The cable (the #1 cause of intermittent blackouts)

Most “keeps going black” cases are a video cable that can’t reliably carry your signal - and it gets worse the higher your resolution and refresh rate. There’s a real physics reason for that, and it’s worth seeing, because it explains why a cable that “works” can still cut to black at 144Hz or 4K.

Why a cable drops to black at 1440p or 4K
The more resolution and refresh you ask for, the more raw bandwidth the signal needs. A cable rated below it - or a marginal, damaged, or uncertified one - passes a basic picture but cuts to black exactly at the top. Bars are approximate uncompressed 8-bit data rates; the dashed lines are what each connector standard can carry.

4K @ 120Hz
~26 Gbps

Read it like this: 1440p144 sits right on HDMI 2.0's ceiling and 4K120 right on DisplayPort 1.4's — the [DisplayPort/HDMI bandwidth calculator](/tools/displayport-hdmi-bandwidth-calculator/) shows whether your resolution and refresh actually fit your cable and port — so a slightly-too-weak or damaged cable that's fine on the desktop drops out the instant you hit those. (DSC - Display Stream Compression - squeezes these down roughly 3:1, which is how a DP 1.4 cable does 4K144 at all; a marginal cable is exactly what fails to hold it.) The fix is almost never a new monitor - it's a properly rated cable.

A marginal cable will pass a basic signal fine but drop out at 144Hz or 4K, cutting the screen to black while it re-syncs.

  • Reseat both ends of the HDMI or DisplayPort cable until they click. DisplayPort especially seats loosely and works its way out.
  • Swap the cable. This is the single highest-yield fix. Use a properly rated VESA-certified DisplayPort cable or an Ultra High Speed HDMI 2.1 cable that’s rated for your resolution and refresh - a cheap or damaged cable is the most common reason a screen blacks out intermittently, and the meter above is why.
  • Try a different port on your graphics card, and make sure the cable is in the graphics card, not the motherboard. If you’re troubleshooting a display Windows doesn’t even list, see second monitor not detected.

Step 2: Recover it instantly, then fix the driver

When the screen is black but the PC is clearly still running, press Windows Key + Ctrl + Shift + B. This restarts the graphics driver on the spot - a brief black screen and a beep, then the picture returns. If that reliably brings it back, your problem is driver- or link-related, not a dead cable.

A blackout-and-recover is often a driver crash-and-recover (Windows calls this a TDR - the display driver stops responding, Windows resets it, and the screen returns after a second). It’s the exact behavior the Tom’s Hardware forums describe for this symptom, and the fix is a clean driver install:

  1. Download the latest driver from NVIDIA, AMD, or Intel.
  2. Remove the current driver cleanly with DDU (Display Driver Uninstaller) in Safe Mode, then install the fresh one.
  3. If it started right after an update, roll the driver back (Device Manager → your GPU → Properties → Driver → Roll Back Driver).

If the blackouts come with a “display driver stopped responding” message, work the full GPU driver crash fix guide - it’s the same underlying TDR problem.

Step 3: Turn off the power settings that cause blackouts

Two Windows power options blank the screen and are easy to miss:

  • Display sleep: Search Edit power plan, and set Turn off the display to a longer time or Never.
  • PCI Express Link State Power Management: in the same Change advanced power settings window, expand PCI Express → Link State Power Management and set it to Off. This setting throttles the link to your graphics card to save power and is a genuine, under-known cause of brief random blackouts on desktops.

The after-sleep case: it's DisplayPort, not the monitor

If the only time it goes black is coming back from sleep - the monitor won’t wake, shows “No Signal,” or your windows have all jumped to another screen - stop suspecting the panel. This is a well-documented quirk of DisplayPort called hot-plug detect: when a DisplayPort monitor sleeps, it drops all signal on the link, so Windows treats it as if you physically unplugged it. On wake, the PC sometimes fails to re-establish the link or reshuffles everything to whatever display it still sees.

Work it in this order - cheapest first:

  • Swap the DisplayPort cable for a known-good, DisplayPort-certified one. A borderline cable makes the re-detect far less reliable, and this alone fixes a surprising number of cases.
  • Check the monitor’s on-screen menu for a setting named DisplayPort deep sleep, DP 1.2/1.4 power, or DDC/CI - toggling that option (off, or to the alternate DP version) changes how the monitor drops the link and often stops the wake blackout. Some Dell and LG panels expose exactly this.
  • Move to HDMI, or use a good active DisplayPort-to-HDMI adapter - HDMI doesn’t kill hot-plug detect the same way, so it sidesteps the whole behavior. It’s the surest workaround if the setting isn’t in your OSD.
  • Blank instead of sleep: set your display to turn off via a blank screensaver with a timeout, rather than the power plan’s “turn off the display,” so the monitor never fully drops the link.

Microsoft has shipped a Windows-side fix for the window-rearranging half of this, but the black-on-wake itself is still best solved at the cable/OSD level above.

Step 4: Heat, power, and a loose GPU (for stay-black and under-load cases)

If the screen stays black until you reboot, or only blacks out under gaming load, it’s usually not the display path:

  • Overheating: a GPU or CPU hitting its thermal limit can trigger a protective blackout. Confirm all fans spin and clear dust from the case and GPU. If temps are high, our safe CPU temperature guide covers what’s normal and when heat is actually the problem.
  • Unstable overclock/undervolt: revert any GPU or CPU tuning (MSI Afterburner, Adrenalin, Ryzen Master) to stock and test. An overclock that’s slightly unstable - or an undervolt that’s too aggressive - produces exactly this black-screen behavior under load.
  • Reseat the GPU: power down, and firmly reseat the graphics card in its PCIe slot and its power connectors. A card that’s crept loose in its slot drops signal intermittently.
  • Power delivery: a failing or underpowered PSU, or a loose PCIe power cable, causes blackouts under load. Make sure the GPU’s power cables are fully seated (including all pins on a 12VHPWR/12V-2x6 connector).

Only in games? Check VRR before you blame the hardware

Here’s a load-related cause almost every other guide misses. If your monitor flashes black or flickers specifically in games and you have G-Sync or FreeSync (VRR) turned on, the odds are it’s the variable-refresh feature itself, not a dying cable or panel.

VRR works by constantly changing the monitor’s refresh interval to match your frame rate. When the frame rate swings hard - and especially when it dips below the monitor’s VRR floor (the low end of its adaptive range, often around 48Hz) - the panel keeps re-timing itself, and OLED and some VA panels show each transition as a brief black flash or brightness flicker. It’s worst in poorly optimized games with uneven frame times, and it looks alarming even though nothing is failing.

Confirm and fix it without buying anything:

  • Cap your frame rate a few fps below the monitor’s maximum, using RTSS (RivaTuner Statistics Server) or the in-game limiter. Staying inside the smooth part of the VRR range is the single most effective fix.
  • Toggle G-Sync/FreeSync off as a test. If the black flashes vanish with VRR disabled, you’ve found it - now you’re tuning, not replacing.
  • Narrow the VRR range with CRU (Custom Resolution Utility) - raising the floor (say, 48-144Hz to 90-144Hz) leaves less brightness gap between refresh steps and cuts the flicker.
  • Update the monitor firmware and GPU driver - panel makers have shipped firmware specifically to reduce VRR flicker.

This is a tuning problem, not a fault. Treat a game-only black flash with VRR on as VRR-first, and you’ll skip a lot of pointless cable-swapping.

The fix order, in one list

  1. Read the rhythm - flicker-and-recover vs stay-black vs under-load vs after-sleep. It tells you which step to start on.
  2. Cable - reseat, then swap for a properly rated cable; confirm it’s in the graphics card. (The #1 cause; see the bandwidth meter.)
  3. Driver - Win+Ctrl+Shift+B to recover, then a DDU clean reinstall (or roll back if a recent update broke it).
  4. Power settings - Turn off display → Never, and PCI Express Link State Power Management → Off.
  5. After-sleep only - it’s DisplayPort hot-plug: certified DP cable, the OSD DP/DDC setting, or move to HDMI.
  6. Heat / power / seating - for stay-black or under-load blackouts: check temps, revert overclocks, reseat the GPU and its power cables.
  7. Games only - if it’s a black flash with VRR on, cap your frame rate and tune the VRR range before suspecting hardware.

Work it in the order your rhythm points to, and you’ll fix the vast majority of random blackouts at step 2 or 3 - a cable swap or a clean driver install - long before “buy a new monitor” is on the table.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my monitor keep going black for a few seconds then come back on?
That specific symptom - a brief blackout that recovers on its own - points to one of three things, in order of likelihood: a video cable that’s loose or can’t hold the bandwidth at your resolution/refresh rate, a graphics driver that crashed and recovered (Windows resets it and the screen returns after a second), or a power-saving setting briefly cutting the link. It is usually NOT a dying monitor. Reseat or swap the cable first, then update or clean-reinstall the GPU driver, then turn off the power-saving options in this guide.
Why does my HDMI (or DisplayPort) keep blacking out?
A cable that can’t reliably carry your signal is the most common cause of intermittent blackouts, and it gets worse the higher your resolution and refresh rate. A marginal HDMI or DisplayPort cable can pass a lower-bandwidth signal fine but drop out at 144Hz or 4K, causing the screen to cut to black and re-sync. Reseat both ends until they click, then test a different, properly rated cable. Also make sure the cable is plugged into your graphics card, not the motherboard, and try a different port to rule out a failing output.
How do I fix a screen that keeps going black without rebooting?
Press Windows Key + Ctrl + Shift + B. This restarts the graphics driver on the spot - the screen goes black for about a second and emits a short beep, then comes back with a fresh display connection. It’s the fastest way to recover a blacked-out screen without a hard reset, and if doing it reliably brings the picture back, that points to a driver or link issue rather than a dead cable or monitor. It’s a recovery step, not a permanent fix - pair it with the driver and cable fixes below.
Can power settings make my monitor go black randomly?
Yes. Two Windows power settings cause random blackouts. First, ‘Turn off the display’ in your power plan will blank the screen after an idle timeout - set it to a longer time or Never. Second, and less obvious, PCI Express ‘Link State Power Management’ can throttle the link to your graphics card and cause brief signal drops: open Edit Power Plan, then Change advanced power settings, then PCI Express, then Link State Power Management and set it to Off. These two together fix a lot of ‘goes black at random’ cases that aren’t hardware at all.
Why does my monitor go black after sleep or when it wakes up?
That’s almost always DisplayPort, not a failing monitor. When a DisplayPort monitor sleeps, it stops all signal on the link, so Windows treats it as physically unplugged - and on wake it sometimes fails to re-detect the display or shuffles your windows to another screen. Fixes, in order: try a different DisplayPort-certified cable, look in the monitor’s on-screen menu for a ‘DisplayPort deep sleep’, ‘DP power’, or DDC/CI option and toggle it, or connect over HDMI (or an active DisplayPort-to-HDMI adapter) to sidestep the behavior entirely. Setting the display to blank via a screensaver instead of sleeping also avoids it.
Why does my gaming monitor go black or flicker only in games?
If it flashes black or flickers specifically in games with G-Sync or FreeSync (VRR) turned on, that’s usually VRR instability, not the cable or a dying panel. When your frame rate swings - especially below the monitor’s VRR floor - the display keeps changing its refresh interval, and OLED and some VA panels show that as a brief black flash or brightness flicker, worse in poorly optimized games with uneven frame times. Cap your frame rate a few fps below the monitor’s maximum (RTSS or the in-game limiter) so it stays inside the smooth VRR range, and as a test, toggle G-Sync/FreeSync off - if the blackouts stop, it’s VRR and you tune it rather than replace anything.
Why does my monitor go black during games specifically?
Blackouts that only happen under load usually mean heat, power, or VRR, not the cable. A GPU or CPU hitting its thermal limit can trigger a protective blackout or a driver reset mid-game - check that all fans are spinning and clear dust from the case and GPU. An unstable overclock or an over-aggressive undervolt does the same thing, so revert any GPU/CPU tuning to stock and test. If it’s a quick black flash rather than a hard cut, suspect VRR flicker first. If it only blacks out in demanding titles and never on the desktop, treat it as a thermal, power, or VRR problem, not a display problem.
Is my monitor dying if it keeps going black?
Usually not. A monitor that blanks and recovers is far more likely to be a cable, driver, or power-setting issue than a failing panel - a truly dying monitor tends to show permanent artifacts, dead zones, backlight problems, or fails to come back at all. Work the cable, driver, and power fixes first. Only after those are ruled out, and especially if the monitor stays black and needs its own power cycle to recover, is the monitor’s internal board or power supply a likely suspect.
