Quick answer

Undervolting lowers the voltage your GPU runs at, cutting heat, power, and fan noise while holding the same performance — and on a power-limited card it can even add a few FPS. On NVIDIA you use the MSI Afterburner voltage-frequency curve; on AMD you set a negative voltage offset in Adrenalin. Both are safe, reversible, and take about ten minutes.

By LK Wood IV · Published 2026-07-24 · ~9 min read · St. Louis County, MO

What undervolting actually does

Every graphics card leaves the factory with more voltage than it strictly needs. AMD and NVIDIA program a voltage-frequency curve that has to keep the worst chip of a given model stable, so most cards run higher voltage at a given clock than their particular silicon requires. Undervolting reclaims that margin: you tell the card to run the same clocks at a lower voltage.

The payoff comes in one of two forms, depending on your card:

  • Lower heat, power, and noise at the same performance. Less voltage means less power turned into heat, so temperatures drop, fans spin slower, and the card is quieter — with the same frame rates.
  • A few more frames. On a card that runs into its power limit under load (common on higher-end GPUs), lowering voltage frees up power budget, and the boost algorithm spends it on higher sustained clocks. That’s why undervolting can come out slightly faster, not slower.

It is one of the highest-value ten-minute tweaks in a PC, it costs nothing, and it’s fully reversible. This guide covers the method for both brands; for card-specific settings and measured results, it links down to our worked examples on a NVIDIA RTX 5080 and an AMD RX 9070 XT.

Before you start: baseline your card

You can’t tell an improvement from a placebo without a reference point. Run a demanding game or a benchmark for 10–15 minutes with a monitoring overlay (HWiNFO64, or AMD’s built-in metrics), and note four numbers under sustained load:

  • Core clock (the sustained clock, not the momentary boost spike)
  • Core voltage (VID / SVI)
  • Power draw (board watts)
  • Temperature — watch the hotspot / junction temperature, not just the edge temperature

Those are the numbers you’re trying to beat: same or higher clock, at lower power and temperature.

NVIDIA: the MSI Afterburner curve

NVIDIA cards are undervolted through MSI Afterburner’s voltage-frequency curve editor — the same tool works across every NVIDIA card, and on AMD cards too. The method:

  1. Download and open MSI Afterburner and press Ctrl+F to open the curve editor — a graph of clock (MHz) against voltage (mV).
  2. Note where your card runs at stock under load (from your baseline). Pick a lower voltage target — around 0.9V (900mV) is a widely used, safe starting point — and drag that point up to your stock sustained clock.
  3. Flatten the curve to the right of your chosen point so the card can’t climb back to higher voltages, then click Apply.
  4. Stress test (below). Once stable, save the profile and enable “apply at startup” so it loads every boot.

The idea is simple: you’re forcing the card to hit its normal clock at a lower voltage, and capping it there. For the full curve-editor walkthrough with measured before/after numbers on a current card, see the RTX 5080 undervolt guide.

AMD: the Adrenalin voltage offset

AMD makes this easier — everything is built into AMD Software: Adrenalin Edition, so there’s no third-party tool. Rather than editing a curve point by point, you apply a negative voltage offset across the whole curve:

  1. Press Alt+R to open Adrenalin, go to Performance → Tuning, and set the Tuning Control to Custom.
  2. Enable GPU Tuning and lower the voltage with a negative offset — start conservative at around -40mV.
  3. Optionally enable Power Tuning and trim the power limit (around -10% as a mild starting cut keeps performance closest to stock while lowering temperatures; deeper cuts trade performance for efficiency).
  4. Apply, stress test, and step the offset deeper (toward -80 to -100mV) as stability allows.

The RX 9070 XT undervolt guide covers the Adrenalin controls, the community-converged offsets, and the efficiency-versus-performance trade in full. Older AMD cards and ASUS cards can also use GPU Tweak III, but the Adrenalin route works on any modern Radeon.

Test for stability (don’t skip this)

An undervolt that looks fine for five minutes and crashes an hour into a game is worse than no undervolt. Test it properly:

  • Run a demanding, real game or a benchmark like 3DMark for at least 15 minutes — longer is better.
  • Watch for crashes, driver timeouts (a black screen that recovers), or artifacts — these mean the voltage is too low for the clock you set. Raise the voltage (NVIDIA) or reduce the negative offset (AMD) slightly and retest.
  • A common trap: an undervolt can pass a heavy all-core stress test but crash at lighter loads, so play normally for a few hours before you trust it.

When a setting survives real use, you’ve found your card’s stable point. Save it.

What to expect

Results vary by chip and by game — no two cards are identical — but the pattern is consistent: lower temperatures and power draw, quieter fans, and either the same or slightly better performance. On efficiency-focused cards the win is mostly thermal and acoustic; on power-limited, higher-end cards, undervolting can recover a few percent of performance on top. Pick your card, follow the method, and test it honestly. Ten minutes buys a cooler, quieter, and often slightly faster GPU, for free.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe to undervolt a GPU?
Yes. Undervolting lowers the voltage the card runs at, which reduces electrical and thermal stress rather than adding it — the opposite of overvolting, so it does not carry the physical-stress risk that overvolting does. The only realistic failure is instability: if you go too low, a game or benchmark crashes, which you fix by raising the voltage slightly and retesting. Every setting lives in software, so resetting the profile or reinstalling the driver returns the card fully to stock at any time.
Does undervolting a GPU give more FPS?
It can, on cards that run into their power limit. When you lower voltage at a given clock, the card draws less power for the same work — and if it was previously capped by its power limit, it now has headroom to hold higher boost clocks, which shows up as a few more frames. On a card that is not power-limited, you instead keep the same performance while running cooler and quieter. Either way you do not lose performance if the undervolt is tuned correctly.
How much should you undervolt a GPU?
There is no single number, because silicon quality varies from card to card — that is exactly why the setting exists. On NVIDIA, a common safe starting point is targeting around 0.9V (900mV) at your stock boost clock and adjusting from there. On AMD, owners typically start with a small negative offset (around -40mV) and step deeper toward -80 to -100mV while testing. Treat any published value as a starting point to test on your own card, never a setting to copy blindly.
How do I undervolt an NVIDIA GPU?
Use MSI Afterburner’s voltage-frequency curve editor (Ctrl+F). Find your stock sustained clock and voltage under load, pick a lower voltage point (around 0.9V is a common target), drag it up to your stock clock, then flatten the curve above it so the card cannot climb to higher voltages. Apply, stress test for 15 minutes, and save the profile with apply-at-startup enabled. Our RTX 5080 undervolt guide walks through the exact curve steps.
How do I undervolt an AMD GPU?
Use AMD Software: Adrenalin Edition — no third-party tool is needed. Press Alt+R, open Performance, then Tuning, choose Custom, enable GPU Tuning, and apply a negative voltage offset (start around -40mV). Optionally lower the power limit for more efficiency, then apply and stress test. Our RX 9070 XT undervolt guide covers the Adrenalin settings and what to expect step by step.
What tools do I need to undervolt my GPU?
For NVIDIA cards, MSI Afterburner (which also works on AMD) plus a monitoring tool like HWiNFO64 and a stress test such as 3DMark or a demanding game. For AMD cards, everything is built into AMD Software: Adrenalin Edition, including tuning and monitoring. You do not need to buy anything — all of these tools are free.
