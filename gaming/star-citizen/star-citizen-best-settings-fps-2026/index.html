Quick answer

Graphics settings are the weakest lever in Star Citizen. The game is bound by CPU single-thread speed, system RAM, NVMe streaming, and the server you land on, so dropping sliders barely moves frame rate. Your biggest real gains come from the renderer and upscaling choice, not from lowering shadows or textures.

If you came here expecting a list of sliders to drop for big FPS gains, here is the honest truth most guides skip: in Star Citizen, graphics settings are the weakest lever you have. In a normal game, lowering settings is the FPS fix. Here it barely moves the needle, because the bottleneck is somewhere your monitor settings cannot reach. The game is limited by CPU single-thread performance, system RAM, NVMe streaming, and above all the server-side simulation. That is not a fault in your PC. It is how this engine works in 2026, and understanding it will save you hours of slider-tweaking that goes nowhere.

So this guide ranks the real levers in order: your hardware floor first, then your renderer and upscaling choice, then the short list of settings that actually help, and finally the part nobody can tune away, the server. Set your expectations there and everything else makes sense.

Why Star Citizen Ignores Your Graphics Card

Star Citizen is primarily CPU-bound, not GPU-bound. The engine spends most of its time processing server state, physics, and NPC AI on the processor, and that work lands hardest on a single core. As one performance tuning guide puts it bluntly, “the CPU is in current state more important than the GPU,” and “even with the fastest GPU on the planet, you won’t achieve high results because, most likely, your CPU will max out before” (citizen-history.com, archived 2026-08-14). The official guidance agrees that high single-core performance matters more than core count (starcitizenhelp.com).

The practical consequence is that even an RTX 4090 will not always peg 120 FPS in this game. Cities and stations, packed with NPCs and physics objects, tank your frame rate far more than open space does, and that drop comes from the CPU, not the GPU. If you want to know whether your processor is the part holding you back versus your graphics card, run your parts through the PC Bottleneck Calculator before you buy anything new. In Star Citizen specifically, a faster single-thread CPU usually beats a bigger GPU.

The Hardware Floor That Actually Sets Your Ceiling

Before any in-game setting matters, three hardware pieces decide your experience.

RAM: 32GB is the sweet spot. CIG’s own published spec sheet lists 32 GB DDR4 at the recommended tier and 16 GB as the bare minimum (RSI system requirements). A test on a 96GB system showed Star Citizen only using 20 to 30GB, confirming that 32GB is plenty and that more does little (citizen-history.com, archived 2026-08-14). 16GB can run the game, but 32GB is the comfort zone most players want.

NVMe SSD: effectively mandatory. Star Citizen streams world data in real time, so a fast NVMe SSD is treated as a hard requirement for a glitch-free experience, not just a nice-to-have. The official guidance is explicit: do not install Star Citizen on a spinning hard drive. Aim for a PCIe Gen 3 or Gen 4 NVMe drive with 3,000 MB/s or higher read speeds, with around 100GB free plus roughly 10GB for the pagefile (starcitizenhelp.com). On a slow drive you get object loading failures and texture pop-in that no graphics setting can fix.

Do not disable your pagefile. This catches a lot of people who have 32GB or 64GB of RAM and assume they no longer need swap. In Star Citizen, “disabling it, even with ample physical memory, invites unnecessary strife.” Set it to System Managed or a minimum of about 16GB, and keep it on the SSD (citizen-history.com, archived 2026-08-14). For the full breakdown of recommended parts and why they matter, see the Star Citizen system requirements guide.

Renderer and Upscaling: Your Second-Biggest Lever

Once your hardware floor is set, the renderer is the next decision. Vulkan, the Gen12 renderer, is the default as of Alpha 4.5 and it addresses “common stuttering issues and enhancing game stability” compared to the older DirectX 11 path. It precompiles shaders instead of generating them just in time, spreads rendering across multiple CPU cores, and raises minimum frame rates while cutting stutter in contested areas (citizen-history.com, archived 2026-08-14). The fallback advice is simple: Vulkan is better on most modern hardware, but if performance is worse for no clear reason, switch to DirectX 11 and test (GagandeepMalhotra gist).

One gotcha worth flagging: some auto-installed copies of vulkan-1.dll have been flagged by Easy Anti-Cheat, which can cause launch or error problems with the Vulkan renderer. If Vulkan refuses to start, RSI maintains a Vulkan troubleshooting article for exactly this. This sits close to the broader anti-cheat launch issues covered in the Star Citizen error 5007 fix guide, so check there if your launch failure looks anti-cheat related.

On upscaling, Star Citizen Alpha 4.5 added support for the DLSS 4 transformer model alongside the Vulkan improvements, and the game supports NVIDIA DLSS, AMD FSR on all GPUs, and Intel XeSS (mmopixel.com). Star Citizen originally gained DLSS and Vulkan support through an NVIDIA Game Ready driver back in 2024, the same cycle that added F1 24 and Hellblade II (tomshardware.com). Quality or Balanced upscaling is one of the most effective FPS boosts on GPU-limited mid-range hardware, but it provides little benefit when you are CPU-bound, which is most of the time in busy areas.

A clear warning on Frame Generation: it is not supported in Star Citizen as of 4.5. Only upscaling, the super resolution part, works; multi-frame generation does not (starcitizen.tools). Do not go hunting for a Frame Gen toggle expecting it to double your FPS. It is not there, and even if it were, it would not help a CPU or server bottleneck.

The Short List of Settings That Actually Move FPS

Most of the graphics menu is noise in this game. These few entries are the ones worth your attention.

  • Planet Volumetric Clouds: Low. This is one of the biggest FPS killers in the game and can cause a 20-plus FPS difference on mid-range hardware (GagandeepMalhotra gist). Drop it first.
  • Object / Object Container View Distance: Medium or High. Ultra “causes significant frame jitter with minimal visual benefit.” Medium or High is the smart trade; some guides go as low as Low for the biggest gain.
  • Gas Clouds: Low. Shading: Medium. Post Effects: Low. Small but real gains.
  • Object Detail: High minimum. Setting it Low “causes surface rendering issues on planets,” so do not push this one below High.
  • Textures: set by VRAM, not by taste. Under 8GB of VRAM, keep textures at Medium to avoid stuttering; 12GB or more can go by preference. The redline matters here: staying under your VRAM cap is smooth, but hitting it, for example 11.2 of 11.2 GB on a 12GB card, creates stutter (GagandeepMalhotra gist).
  • Shadows, Fog, Water Caustics, Video Comms: negligible FPS impact. These are player preference. Tune them for looks, not frames.

A few NVIDIA Control Panel tweaks help too, though only a few matter: expanding the shader cache to about 10GB “has been helpful for many,” and removing motion blur and film grain cleans up the image without taxing the CPU (citizen-history.com, archived 2026-08-14). On Intel 12th-gen and newer CPUs, micro-stuttering tied to efficiency cores is a known issue; disabling E-cores for the game or pinning Star Citizen to P-cores with a tool like Process Lasso can smooth it out.

The Diagnostic That Separates a Bad PC From a Bad Server

This is the differentiator. Before you blame your hardware, open the console with the tilde key, type r_displayInfo 1, and press Enter. You get an FPS, RAM, and latency overlay in the upper-right corner. Higher values, r_displayInfo 2 through 4, show progressively more detail, and r_displayInfo 0 turns it off (guided.news).

Read it like this. If lowering your resolution barely changes FPS, and cities tank far more than space, you are CPU-bound. If FPS jumps when you lower resolution or switch upscaling modes, and GPU usage is consistently high, you are GPU-bound. And the overlay also shows the server. A normal server should run at roughly 4 to 8 FPS; values below about 2 FPS mean a degraded server, and the fix is to switch servers, not to touch a single graphics setting (a Q1 2024 reading, per citizen-history.com, archived 2026-08-14). No client setting changes the server tick. That is the ceiling on your experience, and it is invisible until you measure it.

One more expectation to set: Star Citizen compiles shaders on startup, so very low frame rates for roughly the first 10 minutes after logging in, or after a patch, are normal and not a settings problem (boostroom.com). Clear the shader cache after major updates only. Constant clearing just forces expensive rebuilds.

Where to Go Next

Settings help less in Star Citizen than almost anywhere else, and now you know why and what to actually do about it. If you are still deciding whether your CPU or GPU is the real limiter, run your build through the PC Bottleneck Calculator, since in this game a stronger single-thread CPU beats a bigger graphics card. To plan a build that meets the real hardware floor of 32GB RAM and a fast NVMe drive, start with the Star Citizen system requirements guide. And if your trouble is the game refusing to launch rather than running slowly, the Star Citizen error 5007 fix covers the anti-cheat and Vulkan launch failures that often get mistaken for performance problems.

Frequently asked questions

What are the best Star Citizen settings for FPS in 2026?
The biggest in-game wins are setting Planet Volumetric Clouds to Low, keeping Object Container View Distance at Medium or High instead of Ultra, and turning on upscaling like DLSS, FSR, or XeSS at Quality or Balanced. Gas Clouds Low, Shading Medium, and Post Effects Low also help. But these sliders move far less than they would in a normal game, because Star Citizen is mostly limited by your CPU, system RAM, NVMe streaming, and the server itself. Lowering settings cannot fix a CPU or server bottleneck.
Why is my FPS so low in Star Citizen even with a good GPU?
Star Citizen is frequently CPU-bound, not GPU-bound. The engine processes server state, physics, and NPC AI on the CPU, and high single-core performance matters more than core count or your graphics card. Even an RTX 4090 will not always reach 120 FPS, especially in cities and stations where the CPU maxes out first. On top of that, the server runs its own simulation at roughly 4 to 8 FPS, and a degraded server caps your experience no matter what hardware you own.
Does Star Citizen support DLSS Frame Generation?
No. As of Star Citizen Alpha 4.5, Frame Generation is not supported. The game added support for the DLSS 4 transformer upscaling model in 4.5, and it also supports AMD FSR and Intel XeSS upscaling, but only super resolution (upscaling) works, not multi-frame generation. Upscaling reconstructs a lower-resolution image up to your target resolution and can lift FPS noticeably when you are GPU-bound. It does little when the CPU or server is the limiter, which in Star Citizen is often the case.
Does more RAM raise FPS in Star Citizen?
32GB is the practical sweet spot. CIG’s published spec sheet lists 32 GB DDR4 at the recommended tier and 16 GB as the minimum. A 96GB test system showed Star Citizen only using 20 to 30GB, which confirms 32GB is enough. 16GB can run the game, but 32GB gives a comfort zone many players want. Do not disable the Windows pagefile even with 32GB or more installed, because that causes crashes and instability in this title. Keep it System-Managed or at least 16GB on your SSD.
Should I use Vulkan or DirectX 11 in Star Citizen?
Vulkan, the Gen12 renderer, is the default as of Alpha 4.5 and is better on most modern hardware. It precompiles shaders instead of generating them just in time, spreads rendering work across multiple CPU cores, and reduces stutter in busy areas. If your performance is worse on Vulkan for no clear reason, switch to DirectX 11 as a stability baseline and test again. Note that some auto-installed copies of vulkan-1.dll have been flagged by Easy Anti-Cheat, which can cause launch issues, so check RSI’s Vulkan troubleshooting article if Vulkan will not start.
How do I check if I am CPU-bound or GPU-bound in Star Citizen?
Open the console with the tilde key, type r_displayInfo 1, and press Enter to show an FPS, RAM, and latency overlay in the upper-right corner. Higher numbers like r_displayInfo 2, 3, or 4 add more detail, and r_displayInfo 0 turns it off. Signs you are CPU-limited: lowering resolution barely changes FPS, and cities or stations tank FPS far more than open space. Signs you are GPU-limited: FPS jumps when you lower resolution or change upscaling, and GPU usage stays high. The overlay also exposes a bad server.
